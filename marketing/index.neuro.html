Q2 2026 · active crisis
◆ AI overconfidence 79%

79% of employees feel unprepared to use AI at work. Yet the more AI-literate someone is, the more overconfident they become. Aalto University (N=698) found the classic Dunning-Kruger effect reverses with AI use: higher AI literacy correlated with LOWER metacognitive accuracy. The engineers who seem most AI-savvy may be the worst at catching AI errors.

Bright Horizons 2025; Aalto University, Computers in Human Behavior, 2025
◆ The job flipped, the interview didn't 11.4hrs

Developers now spend 11.4 hours per week reviewing AI-generated code versus 9.8 hours writing new code. Reviewing overtook writing as the single largest time sink in Q1 2026. Yet 77% of developers say the assessments they face don't reflect the skills actually needed for the job. The interview tests puzzle-solving under pressure. The job requires judgment under uncertainty.

Digital Applied 2026 Developer Survey; HackerRank 2025 (n=13,000)

Work sample tests have the highest predictive validity of any hiring method — r = .54 for overall job performance, from an 85-year meta-analysis by Schmidt & Hunter. Yet only 21% of employers use them. 44% still use algorithmic questions that studies show have zero correlation with job success. The science is unanimous. The practice is backwards.

Schmidt & Hunter, Psychological Bulletin 1998; CoderPad 2025

03 · The Signals

Three signals. One assessment. Zero fiction.

Every other platform tests whether a candidate can write code. PIPE tests whether they can do what senior engineers actually do — because in the age of AI, writing is cheap. Judgment is scarce.

01

AI Literacy

Can they evaluate what AI produces?

AI doesn't eliminate the need for engineers. It elevates the required skill from production to evaluation. The U.S. Department of Labor's February 2026 AI Literacy Framework lists "evaluate AI outputs" as a core workforce competency. But no existing hiring platform — HackerRank, CodeSignal, Codility, TestGorilla — tests whether a candidate can catch hallucinations in generated code, spot subtle API misuses, or recognize when a plausible-looking solution is architecturally wrong.

PIPE's AI Implementer Challenge presents candidates with real code generated by an AI agent working on an actual open-source issue. The candidate must review it like a senior engineer: ask questions, push back on assumptions, catch edge cases the AI missed. This isn't a multiple-choice AI quiz. It's a live evaluation of engineering judgment in the human-AI collaboration era.

U.S. DOL AI Literacy Framework, Feb 2026; Long & Magerko CHI 2020; Karat NextGen Rubrics 2026
02

Reading Code

Can they reason about systems they didn't build?

Robert C. Martin established the famous ratio: engineers spend 10:1 more time reading code than writing it. Academic research (LaToza et al.) puts the figure at 58-70% of all developer time spent comprehending code. Will Larson, after interviewing 20+ Staff+ engineers at Google, Stripe, Slack, and Uber, summarized their unanimous view: "None write as much as they used to. You'll be reading a ton of your coworkers' code and doing a fair number of code reviews."

Yet the standard technical interview tests writing in isolation — often algorithmic puzzles that have zero correlation with job success. Code comprehension recruits different brain regions than code construction (eLife, 2020; Nature, 2024). Reading is a distinct, measurable expertise. And it's the expertise that separates junior engineers from senior ones.

PIPE's Code Review Challenge drops candidates into a real open-source library matched to their skills. They must read unfamiliar architecture, understand conventions they didn't create, and provide actionable feedback on a real PR. Because that's the job.

Robert C. Martin, Clean Code; LaToza et al.; Will Larson, Staff Engineer; Ivanova et al., eLife 2020; Schmidt & Hunter 1998
03

E2E Open Source

Can they ship in a real codebase?

SWE-bench — the Princeton-built benchmark for evaluating AI systems on real software engineering — uses 2,294 actual GitHub issues from 12 popular Python repositories. Even Claude 2 solves only 1.96% of them. Real issues are genuinely hard because they require navigating unfamiliar conventions, coordinating multi-file changes, and reasoning about code you didn't write in systems you didn't design.

This is exactly what a senior engineer does on day one. PIPE matches candidates to real open-source libraries aligned with their actual skills — not toy problems, not take-home projects that create adverse selection (62% completion rate, 20% of Dropbox candidates simply didn't finish). The candidate reviews a real PR, then implements a fix in the same real codebase, working through the full workflow: read, review, implement, test, document.

Work sample tests have the highest predictive validity of any hiring method (r = .54). The closest thing to a work sample in software engineering is a real PR in a real repo. That's what PIPE measures.

SWE-bench, Princeton ICLR 2024; Schmidt & Hunter 1998; interviewing.io; Slack Engineering 2019

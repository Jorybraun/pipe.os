## Findings by sub-question

### Q1 — EVP definition and empirical impact

Brett Minchington, widely credited as the originator of the modern Employer Value Proposition framework, defines EVP as "a set of associations and offerings provided by an organization in return for the skills, capabilities and experiences an employee brings to the organization" (Minchington 2005, cited in S1). This conceptualization frames EVP as a reciprocal exchange rather than a unilateral employer promise, which has direct implications for intake design: recruiters must extract what the organization *gives* in exchange for what the role *demands*.

Gartner, which formalized the EVP concept in the early 2000s through the Corporate Leadership Council, identifies five major EVP categories encompassing 38 distinct attributes: **Rewards** (compensation, bonuses, equity), **Opportunity** (career development, advancement, learning), **Organisation** (culture, values, reputation), **People** (leadership, team dynamics, collaboration), and **Work** (flexibility, autonomy, meaningful tasks) (S3). This taxonomy provides a structural checklist for intake conversations: has the recruiter touched all five categories, or are they overweighting Rewards and underweighting People and Work?

The empirical business impact of a well-articulated EVP is substantial and measurable. Gartner research shows that organizations with strong EVPs can reduce the compensation premium needed to attract talent by **50%** and reach **50% deeper into passive candidate pools**—meaning they can attract candidates who aren't actively looking (S3). LinkedIn Talent Solutions data corroborates this, finding that companies with strong employer brands see a **43–50% decrease in cost-per-hire** and receive **more than double the number of applicants per job posting** compared to companies with weaker brands (S2, S27, S28). Critically, **65% of candidates discontinue a hiring process due to an unattractive EVP** (S3), meaning missing EVP extraction during intake creates mid-funnel drop-off that no amount of sourcing can fix.

Offer acceptance is the sharpest test of EVP effectiveness. Korn Ferry's 2024 recruiter survey found that **42% of recruiters** identified company reputation and EVP—rather than position details, pay, or benefits—as the **primary determinant of candidates' acceptance decisions** (S17). This finding inverts the traditional intake model: if recruiters spend 80% of intake time on role requirements and 20% on "why would someone want this," they are optimizing the wrong variable.

Gartner also reports that organizations with well-managed EVPs can decrease annual employee turnover by up to **69%** and increase new-hire commitment by nearly **30%** (S3). This suggests EVP extracted during intake has a dual downstream effect: it improves both candidate conversion *and* retention, because candidates who accept offers based on accurate EVP expectations are less likely to experience reality shock and quit.

### Q2 — EVP source material and how to extract it

The practitioner consensus across multiple EVP consulting frameworks (Rally Recruitment Marketing S18, S19; Blu Ivy Group S34; Korn Ferry S17) is that **the best EVPs are shaped by listening to existing employees** via structured qualitative and quantitative research. The gold-standard extraction process involves employee engagement surveys (quantitative validation), focus groups (qualitative depth), exit interviews (loss analysis), stay interviews (retention drivers), and onboarding feedback (early-stage reality checks). However, this multi-month, multi-stakeholder process is not available to a recruiter conducting a single 60-minute intake call with a hiring manager.

The shortcut available during intake is to **ask the hiring manager to channel the voice of current employees**. Rally's EVP methodology emphasizes that external facilitation of focus groups and interviews yields richer, more honest insights than HR-led sessions, because employees withhold true feelings when power dynamics are present (S18). A recruiter, positioned as an external partner rather than internal HR, may be able to extract more candid EVP material from a hiring manager than the hiring manager would volunteer in an internal strategy session. This suggests the recruiter should explicitly frame intake questions as "What would your team members say if I asked them...?" rather than "What do you think...?"

The distinction between **qualitative** and **quantitative** research is load-bearing. Qualitative methods (interviews, focus groups) yield the "why" behind employee experience and provide narrative texture—concrete anecdotes, metaphors, illustrative stories. Quantitative methods (surveys, Net Promoter Score) validate findings across the organization and measure prevalence (S18, S19). A single intake call is inherently qualitative, which means the recruiter's output is **depth, not breadth**. The recruiter cannot determine if "we have great work-life balance" is universally true or an outlier opinion; they can only capture the hiring manager's perception and supporting anecdotes. This limitation should be disclosed: recruiter-extracted EVP is **single-source, unverified** until triangulated across multiple hires, employees, or other stakeholders.

BCG's employer brand case study provides a concrete example of research-driven EVP development. BCG conducted extensive employee interviews and leadership consultations to identify their core EVP pillars, then validated the narrative through internal testing workshops where employees brought the EVP to life with real-world examples (S6, S7). The intake analog: rather than asking "What's your culture?" (which yields generic platitudes), ask "Can you describe a moment in the last month where someone on your team exemplified your culture?" The latter yields concrete proof points recruiters can repeat verbatim.

LinkedIn Talent data shows that **companies with Career Pages see 8× more job applications** and recruiters see **2.6× higher InMail response rates** from candidates who visited those pages (S2). This finding underscores that EVP content—team stories, employee testimonials, success anecdotes—has measurable top-of-funnel impact. The intake call is the primary sourcing moment for that content. If the recruiter leaves intake without 3–5 concrete stories, they have failed to extract the highest-leverage sales ammunition.

A critical nuance: EVP consultants distinguish between **company-level EVP** (what it's like to work here in general) and **role-specific EVP** (what makes *this particular job* compelling). Role-specific EVP is often more persuasive for hard-to-fill positions, especially senior or specialized roles (S3, multiple employer brand sources). During intake, the recruiter must extract both: "What makes this company a great place to work?" (company EVP) and "What makes *this role* uniquely compelling compared to the same title at a competitor?" (role EVP). The second question is harder and yields more differentiated sales ammunition.

### Q3 — Dual-purpose probe design (PRIMARY DELIVERABLE)

A dual-purpose probe is a single question that simultaneously extracts (a) role requirements and success criteria (discovery) and (b) compelling narrative material the recruiter can pitch to candidates (sales ammunition). The design pattern is straightforward: **ask for a concrete past example, then unpack what made it successful**. This structure reveals both the competencies the hiring manager values (requirements) and the story the recruiter can retell (pitch).

The foundational insight from intake methodology literature (S20–S24) is that the best intake questions are **open-ended, prompt detailed descriptions, and avoid yes/no answers**. "Do you need someone with Python experience?" yields a binary data point. "Tell me about the last technical problem this team solved and how the solution came together" yields a story, a protagonist, a challenge, a resolution, and an implicit set of valued competencies (technical depth, collaboration, problem-solving under ambiguity, etc.).

Here are **10 worked examples** of dual-purpose probes, drawn from practitioner sources and extended with synthesis across Q1–Q2 findings:

#### 1. **"Tell me about someone who joined this team in the last year and was really successful. What did they do in their first 60–90 days?"**

- **Discovery signal extracted:** Success criteria, onboarding expectations, time-to-productivity, valued behaviors, team integration patterns.
- **Sales ammunition extracted:** Concrete anecdote of a real person who thrived; candidate can visualize themselves in that story; demonstrates team is good at onboarding.
- **Source:** S23 (GoodTime 2024), S24 (HireTruffle 2024), first-90-days framework literature.
- **Why it works:** Hiring managers default to abstract requirements ("we need a strong communicator"). Asking for a real person forces concrete behavioral examples. The recruiter can pitch: "When Sarah joined six months ago, she spent her first 30 days shadowing every step of the release process, then proposed a new CI/CD pipeline that cut deploy time by 40%. That's the kind of early impact this team rewards."

#### 2. **"What would success look like for this person six months in? What's the one thing they'd accomplish that would make you say 'this was a great hire'?"**

- **Discovery signal extracted:** Primary success metric, timeline expectations, prioritization (what matters *most*), hiring manager's internal definition of "great."
- **Sales ammunition extracted:** Clear, concrete goal candidate can evaluate ("Is this the kind of problem I want to solve?"); demonstrates role has tangible impact.
- **Source:** S20, S21, S22, S24 (intake methodology consensus).
- **Why it works:** Hiring managers often list 15 responsibilities. This probe forces prioritization. The recruiter can pitch: "In six months, if you've rebuilt the payment reconciliation system and reduced manual errors by 80%, you'll have solved the #1 pain point for the finance team. That's the hill this role exists to take."

#### 3. **"What's a win for this role in the first 90 days?"**

- **Discovery signal extracted:** Near-term milestones, ramp expectations, early-stage autonomy vs. mentorship, quick-win opportunities.
- **Sales ammunition extracted:** Candidate can assess realistic timeline; demonstrates team values early contributions.
- **Source:** S24 (HireTruffle 2024), first-90-days framework.
- **Why it works:** Reveals whether the role expects immediate impact (senior hire, urgent need) or gradual ramp (junior hire, learning curve). Recruiter can pitch: "Your first 90 days: learn the codebase, ship one feature end-to-end, present your observations to the product team. They don't expect you to be an expert on day one—they expect curiosity and follow-through."

#### 4. **"Can you walk me through the last big project this team shipped? What went well, and what was harder than expected?"**

- **Discovery signal extracted:** Team workflows, technical stack in action, collaboration patterns, risk tolerance, post-mortem culture, conflict resolution.
- **Sales ammunition extracted:** Concrete product/project story; demonstrates team ships real things; transparency about friction (RJP).
- **Source:** Synthesis of storytelling best practices (employer brand literature) + RJP literature (S12).
- **Why it works:** Asking "what was harder than expected" is a transparent-friction RJP probe disguised as a discovery question. Hiring managers who answer honestly ("we underestimated database migration time and worked two weekends") give recruiters ammunition to pitch realism, not fantasy. Per Earnest et al. (2011), honesty builds trust and reduces turnover (S12).

#### 5. **"Tell me about the last engineer who left this team. Why did they leave, and what did you learn?"**

- **Discovery signal extracted:** Attrition patterns, retention risks, culture fit criteria (inverse), career progression gaps, compensation competitiveness.
- **Sales ammunition extracted:** Transparency signal (team reflects on losses); proof of learning culture; RJP about what this role *isn't* good for.
- **Source:** Exit interview literature (S18, S19); RJP meta-analyses (S9–S12).
- **Why it works:** Most recruiters avoid this question. It's the highest-signal RJP probe available. If the answer is "they left for a 30% raise and we can't match that," the recruiter knows compensation is a knockout factor and can pre-screen. If the answer is "they wanted to manage people and we're an IC-track team," the recruiter can pitch: "This is the place for deep technical work, not people management. If that's what you want, this is perfect. If not, let's not waste your time."

#### 6. **"What can this role offer that a 500-person company can't?"**

- **Discovery signal extracted:** Differentiation criteria, autonomy level, decision-making proximity, flat hierarchy, cross-functional exposure.
- **Sales ammunition extracted:** Startup/small-company EVP positioning (S29, S30); anti-big-company pitch for candidates fleeing bureaucracy.
- **Source:** S29 (Daggerfinn 2024), S30 (Entrepreneur 2016), startup employer brand literature.
- **Why it works:** Forces hiring manager to articulate *relative* advantage, not absolute. Recruiter can pitch: "At a 500-person company, you'd spend six months getting budget approval. Here, if you convince the CEO in a 20-minute Slack call, you ship it next week. That's the trade-off: less process, more ownership."

#### 7. **"Where is the product in its lifecycle right now, and what does that mean for this hire's day-to-day?"**

- **Discovery signal extracted:** Product-market fit stage, role ambiguity, build vs. maintain vs. scale, roadmap clarity, pivot risk.
- **Sales ammunition extracted:** Product-stage narrative (S26); candidate can self-select for "early chaos" vs. "mature optimization."
- **Source:** S26 (SignalFire 2024), startup recruiting literature.
- **Why it works:** Product stage determines everything—tech stack, team size, customer maturity, funding runway, role stability. Recruiter can pitch: "We're post-Series A, 10K users, scaling from 'it works' to 'it works at 100K users.' You'll rewrite the notification system, not invent it from scratch. If you want greenfield, that's not us. If you want to solve scale problems, this is it."

#### 8. **"How much ownership will this person have over technical decisions? Can you give me an example of a decision they'd make solo vs. one they'd need buy-in for?"**

- **Discovery signal extracted:** Autonomy boundaries, approval chains, decision-making culture, trust level, micromanagement risk.
- **Sales ammunition extracted:** Autonomy pitch (S25: autonomy increases motivation by 32%); concrete decision examples.
- **Source:** S25 (Holloway 2024), autonomy motivation research.
- **Why it works:** "Autonomy" is abstract. "You'll choose the A/B testing framework solo; you'll need product approval to deprecate a feature" is concrete. Recruiter can pitch to senior candidates: "You own the how, not just the what. If you're someone who needs to debate every implementation detail with a committee, you'll hate this. If you want to be trusted, you'll love it."

#### 9. **"Who will this person work with most closely, and can you tell me about one of those people—how they work, what they care about?"**

- **Discovery signal extracted:** Collaboration model, team personalities, interpersonal dynamics, reporting structure, peer expectations.
- **Sales ammunition extracted:** Humanizes the team; candidate gets a sense of *who* they'd work with, not just *what* they'd work on.
- **Source:** Synthesis of team culture discovery literature + employer brand storytelling (employee testimonials as highest-value content, S2).
- **Why it works:** "You'll work with Sarah, our lead product designer. She's obsessive about user research—she'll push you to question every assumption, which some engineers find annoying and others find energizing." That's a pre-qualifier. Candidates who hate being challenged will self-select out. Candidates who want that will lean in.

#### 10. **"What's one thing about this role or team that might surprise a candidate—something that's not obvious from the job description?"**

- **Discovery signal extracted:** Hidden requirements, non-obvious friction, unexpected perks, cultural quirks, unstated expectations.
- **Sales ammunition extracted:** Differentiation hook; RJP transparency; "insider knowledge" that makes candidate feel informed.
- **Source:** RJP literature (S9–S13), transparent friction research.
- **Why it works:** Surfaces the non-obvious. Examples: "You'll be on-call one week per month, but we pay $500/week on-call bonus and you can work from anywhere that week" (friction + compensation lever). Or: "You'll present to the executive team monthly, which is rare for an IC role—some people love the visibility, some find it stressful." Recruiter can pitch both the upside and the reality.

#### Dual-purpose probe design principles (synthesis):

1. **Ask for past examples, not hypotheticals.** "Tell me about the last hire" > "What are you looking for?"
2. **Force prioritization.** "What's the *one* thing that would make this a great hire?" > "What are the requirements?"
3. **Surface friction explicitly.** "What was harder than expected?" "Why did the last person leave?" Honesty builds trust (S12).
4. **Ask for concrete decisions/actions.** "What decisions would they make solo?" > "Do they have autonomy?"
5. **Request stories with characters.** "Tell me about Sarah, the lead designer" > "They'll work with a designer."
6. **Probe for differentiation.** "What can this role offer that a big company can't?" > "What's your culture?"
7. **Extract product/project narratives.** "Walk me through the last big project" > "What's the tech stack?"
8. **Validate across Gartner's 5 EVP categories.** Ensure questions touch Rewards, Opportunity, Organisation, People, Work—not just Rewards.

### Q4 — Small-company and startup EVP extraction

The EVP literature is overwhelmingly enterprise-focused (Gartner, BCG, Universum surveys target 500+ headcount organizations), but small companies (<50 employees) and startups face a different extraction challenge and possess different EVP assets. The founder's intuition that small companies have *richer per-employee EVP evidence but less formal extraction process* is supported by practitioner literature, though empirical validation is thin (no large-N studies of startup EVP methods were found).

Daggerfinn's 2024 guide to startup employer branding identifies **five structural advantages** small companies have over enterprises: (1) direct access to the CEO/founder, (2) high autonomy and low bureaucracy, (3) minimal office politics, (4) flexible work arrangements, and (5) cross-functional exposure (employees wear multiple hats, learn faster) (S29). These are *inherent* EVP attributes that require no formal program—they exist by virtue of size. The intake challenge is to **make them concrete and specific** rather than generic.

SignalFire's startup recruiting guide emphasizes that **the employer brand narrative is virtually identical to the investor/customer pitch**, but must be "tweaked to think of it from the employee's perspective" (S26). This suggests a dual-purpose shortcut: recruiters can ask hiring managers to repurpose their Series A deck. "You told investors you're solving X problem for Y customer—how does this hire contribute to that mission?" This surfaces the **product-stage EVP narrative** (early-stage chaos vs. post-PMF scaling) and **mission/customer-impact story** (SALES-4: mission matters more than compensation to most candidates).

The **authenticity advantage** is a recurring theme in small-company EVP research. Candidates are increasingly skeptical of polished employer brand marketing and want to hear from employees, not recruiters or marketers (S29, employer brand storytelling literature). Small companies can offer **direct employee access** as an EVP differentiator: "Before you accept, we'll set up a 30-minute coffee chat with anyone on the team—your choice." This is prohibitively expensive for a 5,000-person company but trivial for a 15-person startup. The intake probe: "Can I connect candidates with a current team member for an informal conversation?" If the hiring manager says yes, the recruiter has a differentiation lever. If no, that's a yellow flag (team culture may not be as strong as claimed).

Entrepreneur magazine's 2016 guide to low-cost employer branding for startups identifies **five tactics** that require minimal budget: (1) employee social media takeovers (authenticity at scale), (2) half-day photo shoots to create 12 months of visual assets, (3) Glassdoor profile management, (4) Google My Business optimization, and (5) LinkedIn content cadence (S30). These tactics are downstream of intake: the recruiter must extract the *raw material* (employee stories, team wins, founder vision) that feeds the content machine.

A critical insight from startup employer brand research: **most startups fail at employer branding because they don't post enough content or participate in enough conversations** (S30). The bottleneck is not budget—it's content generation. If the recruiter extracts 3–5 concrete stories per intake call (successful hire anecdote, recent project win, team culture moment, founder vision, friction transparency), and the company conducts 10 intake calls per year, that's 30–50 stories. That's sufficient raw material for a year of LinkedIn posts, candidate emails, and Career Page copy. The intake call is a **content pipeline**, not just a requirements session.

The small-company EVP disadvantage: **lack of formal career pathways, benefits scale, and brand recognition**. A 15-person startup cannot offer "10 different career tracks" or "best-in-class parental leave" the way Google can. The intake must surface what the company *can* offer (equity upside, early-stage impact, direct CEO mentorship, fast learning curve) and be transparent about what it *cannot* offer (established 401(k) match, tuition reimbursement, global mobility). This is RJP applied to company stage, not just role. The probe: "What's one thing a candidate might expect from a big company that we don't have, and what do we offer instead?" Forces honesty and differentiation simultaneously.

### Q5 — Sales-ammunition categories

Based on Holloway's candidate motivator research (S25), Burnett Specialists' candidate expectation studies (S31), CareerPlug's 2025 candidate experience data (S32), and BCG's EVP framework (S6), the **five core sales-ammunition categories** that actually move candidates are:

#### 1. **Compensation & equity transparency**

**Evidence:** 47% of job seekers expect to learn about salary before applying; job posts with clear pay attract more applicants and build trust (S32). Only 44% of millennials prioritize salary, but *all* candidates want honesty about it (S25, S31). BCG identifies compensation as one of the five EVP pillars but emphasizes it's table stakes, not differentiator (S6).

**Intake extraction:** "What's the approved compensation range for this role, and is there equity/bonus upside?" If hiring manager won't share range, that's a red flag (candidate will ask eventually; hiding it burns trust). Recruiter can pitch: "Base is $140–160K depending on experience, plus 0.15% equity vesting over four years. If you're expecting $200K, let's not waste time. If you're at $130K and looking for growth, this is a meaningful step up."

**Dual-purpose note:** This question also reveals budget constraints (discovery) and whether compensation is competitive (sales feasibility).

#### 2. **Mission, purpose, and customer impact**

**Evidence:** Candidates increasingly evaluate "why does this work matter?" and "who benefits when we succeed?" Mission and culture matter more than compensation to most candidates (S25, S31). Purpose can be grand (societal impact) or local (supporting peers, serving customers well) (S25).

**Intake extraction:** "Why does this work matter? When this team succeeds, who's better off?" Example answer: "We're building the payment system for gig workers who get paid weekly instead of monthly. When it works, a DoorDash driver can pay rent on time instead of taking out a payday loan." That's a mission story the recruiter can pitch verbatim.

**Dual-purpose note:** Also reveals product positioning and customer segment (discovery).

#### 3. **Growth, learning, and career development**

**Evidence:** 83% of employees see learning/development as a *vital factor* behind employer choice (S25). This is the #2 candidate priority after compensation across multiple studies. BCG identifies "internal career mobility" as a top EVP component (S6).

**Intake extraction:** "What does a typical learning/growth trajectory look like on this team? Can you give me an example of someone who grew significantly in the last year?" Example: "Jenna joined as a mid-level engineer two years ago. She led the API redesign, presented at our engineering all-hands, then moved into a tech lead role. She's now mentoring two junior engineers."

**Dual-purpose note:** Reveals mentorship availability, promotion velocity, and whether growth is real or rhetorical (discovery).

#### 4. **Autonomy, ownership, and decision-making authority**

**Evidence:** Autonomy increases intrinsic motivation by up to 32%, especially for knowledge workers (S25). When employees have autonomy, they feel trusted and empowered, boosting productivity and satisfaction (S31). This is a *differentiator*, especially for senior hires fleeing micromanagement.

**Intake extraction:** "How much ownership will this person have over technical decisions? Give me an example of a decision they'd make solo vs. one requiring buy-in." Example: "They'll own the data pipeline architecture end-to-end. They'll need product approval to change what data we collect, but not *how* we collect it."

**Dual-purpose note:** Reveals approval chains and micromanagement risk (discovery).

#### 5. **Team dynamics, culture, and leadership quality**

**Evidence:** Candidates want to feel connected to the organization and look for companies with strong mission, inclusive culture, and authentic leadership (S31). 66% of candidates said positive candidate experience influenced their decision to accept an offer (S32). "Culture fit" is vague; "tell me about the people I'd work with" is concrete.

**Intake extraction:** "Who will this person work with most closely? Tell me about one of them—how they work, what they care about." Example: "You'll pair with Marcus, our senior backend engineer. He's been here three years, knows the system inside-out, loves mentoring, and will challenge every shortcut you propose. If you want someone who'll rubber-stamp your ideas, that's not him. If you want to level up, he's the guy."

**Dual-purpose note:** Reveals team personalities and collaboration culture (discovery).

#### Bonus category (startup-specific): **Product stage and early-impact opportunity**

**Evidence:** Startup candidates evaluate "where is the product in its lifecycle?" and "will my work matter?" (S26). Early-stage roles offer greenfield building; post-PMF roles offer scale challenges. Mismatch causes attrition.

**Intake extraction:** "Where is the product right now—early prototype, post-launch, scaling, mature? What does that mean for this hire's day-to-day?" Recruiter can pitch: "We're post-Series A, 50K users, scaling infra from 'works for us' to 'works for everyone.' You'll rebuild, not build from scratch. If you want 0-to-1, that's not us. If you want 1-to-100, this is it."

**Candidate appeal:** Startups cannot compete on brand or benefits, but they *can* compete on impact velocity. Recruiter must extract the product-stage story to unlock this lever.

### Q6 — Transparent friction and Realistic Job Previews

The Realistic Job Preview (RJP) literature, spanning four major meta-analyses (Premack & Wanous 1985, McEvoy & Cascio 1985, Phillips 1998, Earnest et al. 2011), provides the strongest empirical foundation for answering whether recruiters should surface friction (e.g., on-call rotations, weekend deploys, ambiguous scope) during intake and candidate pitches.

**Core finding (Earnest et al. 2011):** A meta-analysis of 52 studies (n ≈ 17,000) found that **enhanced perceptions of organizational honesty is the primary mechanism** by which RJPs reduce voluntary turnover (S12). In other words, RJPs don't work by lowering expectations (the Premack & Wanous 1985 hypothesis, S9)—they work by *building trust*. When candidates believe the organization is being honest about friction, they trust the organization more, feel less betrayed when friction materializes, and are less likely to quit.

**Effect size:** RJPs presented orally in face-to-face meetings (the intake call format) are more effective than written or video RJPs at reducing turnover (S12). Firms implementing RJPs see an average **35% reduction in 90-day turnover**, with some organizations reporting **50% reduction in early quits** (S13). Phillips' 1998 meta-analysis found RJPs related to lower attrition, lower voluntary turnover, and higher performance (S11).

**The honesty mechanism:** When hiring managers candidly describe friction during intake ("this role requires on-call one week per month, and we had two 2am incidents last quarter"), they signal organizational honesty. The recruiter can then position that friction *positively* to candidates: "Yes, there's on-call, but here's how the team handles it—$500/week on-call bonus, you can work remotely that week, and incidents average 1–2 per quarter, not nightly. If you've been on-call at a startup with no process and no comp, this is a huge upgrade. If you've never done on-call and don't want to start, let's not waste your time." That's an RJP that builds trust *and* pre-qualifies.

**The reality-shock problem:** 48% of employees report leaving a job because the reality didn't match what they were told during hiring (S13). This is the cost of *not* doing RJPs during intake. When recruiters overpromise or hide friction ("the team works normal hours"—true on average, false during release weeks), candidates experience reality shock, feel misled, and quit. The intake call is the recruiter's opportunity to extract friction *from the hiring manager* so they can position it honestly to candidates.

**When to surface friction:** The RJP literature is unambiguous—**earlier is better**. RJPs delivered during recruitment (before offer acceptance) are more effective than those delivered post-hire (S12, S13). This means the intake call should explicitly probe for friction, and the recruiter should surface it in the first conversation with candidates, not after they've invested in multiple interviews.

**The dual-purpose friction probe:** "What's one thing about this role or team that might surprise a candidate—something that's not obvious from the job description?" (see Q3, probe #10). Or: "What was harder than expected on the last big project?" (Q3, probe #4). These extract friction (discovery) and give the recruiter RJP ammunition (sales positioning). Hiring managers who answer honestly ("we underestimated migration time and worked two weekends") enable the recruiter to pitch: "This team ships fast, which sometimes means crunch weeks. Last quarter they worked two weekends during a database migration. If you need strict 9-to-5, this isn't it. If you're okay with occasional sprints when it matters, you'll fit."

**What the literature does NOT say:** There is no evidence that surfacing friction *reduces* candidate interest, provided the friction is (a) honestly framed, (b) contextualized (how often, why it happens, how it's mitigated), and (c) positioned as a trade-off rather than a flaw. "We have on-call" (unframed) is a negative. "We have on-call one week per month, with $500 bonus and remote flexibility, because we run a 24/7 service—if you want to work on something that matters enough to run 24/7, this is the trade-off" (framed) is a qualifier, not a deterrent.

### Q7 — Company EVP vs. role EVP

The employer brand literature distinguishes between **company-level EVP** (what it's like to work at this organization in general) and **role-specific EVP** (what makes *this particular job* compelling). The distinction matters because candidates evaluate both, and recruiters who conflate them produce generic pitches.

**Company EVP** is the aggregate employee experience across all roles, geographies, and functions. It's what Glassdoor reviews, employer brand videos, and Career Pages typically communicate. Gartner's five pillars (Rewards, Opportunity, Organisation, People, Work) are company-level constructs (S3). BCG's global employer brand refresh centered on company EVP: "clarity around purpose, collaboration, and development" applicable to consultants, analysts, and support staff alike (S6, S7).

**Role EVP** is the localized value proposition of a specific job. It answers: "What makes *this role* uniquely compelling compared to the same title at a competitor?" Role EVP is more differentiated, more concrete, and often more persuasive, especially for hard-to-fill or senior positions. Multiple sources (S3, employer brand literature) note that organizations can tailor EVP messaging by function (engineering vs. sales), skill set (ML engineer vs. frontend engineer), and career stage (junior vs. senior).

**Which matters more?** The answer depends on seniority and scarcity. For **junior hires**, company EVP often dominates: they're evaluating "Is this a good place to start my career?" and care about brand reputation, learning culture, and career pathways. For **senior hires**, role EVP often dominates: they're evaluating "Is this the *specific problem* I want to spend the next two years solving?" and care about technical challenge, team quality, autonomy, and impact. A senior ML engineer choosing between two offers will weigh "Do I want to work on recommendations or fraud detection?" more heavily than "Do I want to work at Company A or Company B?"

**Intake implications:** The recruiter must extract *both* during intake and synthesize them for candidates. Company EVP questions: "What makes this company a great place to work overall?" "What's your culture?" "Why did *you* join?" Role EVP questions: "What makes *this role* unique compared to the same title at a competitor?" "What will this person work on that they couldn't work on elsewhere?" "What's the *specific* impact this hire will have?"

**Example synthesis:** "Company EVP: We're a 50-person startup, post-Series A, mission is to make healthcare affordable for gig workers. Team is tight-knit, low bureaucracy, CEO is accessible, equity upside is real. Role EVP: You'll rebuild the claims processing pipeline from scratch—right now it's manual and breaks weekly. If you want to own something end-to-end, this is it. If you want to join a mature system and optimize, that's not us."

**Evidence gap:** No large-N empirical studies directly compare company EVP vs. role EVP effectiveness in driving offer acceptance. The distinction is practitioner consensus, not peer-reviewed finding. However, the logic is sound and grounded in candidate decision-making research (S25, S31, S32).

### Q8 — Intake surfacing vs. employer-brand workshops

EVP consulting firms (Rally, Blu Ivy Group, Korn Ferry) recommend **formal, multi-month EVP development processes** involving employee surveys (n=100+), focus groups (6–8 sessions), leadership interviews, external benchmarking, and validation workshops (S17, S18, S19, S34). These processes take 12–18 months and cost $50K–$500K depending on organizational size. The output is a comprehensive EVP framework with validated messaging, proof points, and activation plans.

A **single 60-minute recruiter intake call** with a hiring manager is not a substitute for this process. It is a *shortcut* with known limitations:

1. **Single-source, unverified:** The hiring manager's perception of EVP may not reflect employee reality. Exit interviews, stay interviews, and engagement surveys provide ground truth; intake calls provide one stakeholder's opinion.

2. **Qualitative depth, not quantitative breadth:** Intake yields rich anecdotes and concrete stories (high value for candidate pitches) but cannot validate prevalence. "We have great work-life balance" may be true for the hiring manager's team and false company-wide.

3. **Role-specific, not company-wide:** Intake extracts role EVP and team EVP, not necessarily company EVP. A recruiter conducting 10 intakes across 10 hiring managers will surface 10 localized EVPs, which may or may not cohere into a unified company narrative.

4. **No external benchmarking:** EVP workshops compare the organization's offerings against competitors (TalentNeuron sentiment analysis, Glassdoor competitive data, salary benchmarks). Intake calls do not. The hiring manager may believe "we pay competitively," but without market data, the recruiter cannot validate.

5. **Faster, cheaper, good enough:** Intake surfacing takes 60 minutes and costs $0 (recruiter's time is sunk). EVP workshops take 12 months and cost $100K+. For a 15-person startup hiring 5 roles/year, the workshop is overkill. For a 5,000-person enterprise hiring 500 roles/year, intake alone is insufficient.

**What intake does well:** Extracts **concrete, role-specific sales ammunition** (stories, anecdotes, success examples) that formal EVP workshops often miss. Workshops produce frameworks and pillars; intake produces *proof points*. BCG's EVP refresh included internal testing workshops where employees brought the EVP to life with real-world examples (S7)—this is what intake does by default. The hiring manager who says "Jenna joined two years ago, led the API redesign, and got promoted to tech lead" is providing a proof point the recruiter can use immediately.

**What intake misses:** Validation, coherence, external benchmarking, and company-wide narrative. If the recruiter extracts "great work-life balance" from one hiring manager and "we work weekends during crunch" from another, the company has an EVP coherence problem that intake alone cannot solve.

**Practitioner recommendation (synthesis):** For companies <100 employees, intake surfacing is sufficient for initial EVP extraction. Recruiters should conduct intake across *multiple* hiring managers (5–10) and look for patterns. Recurring themes (e.g., "autonomy," "fast learning," "direct CEO access") become the informal company EVP. Contradictions (e.g., "we have work-life balance" vs. "we work weekends") should be flagged for leadership to resolve. For companies >100 employees or those struggling with hiring/retention, a formal EVP workshop is justified. Intake surfacing should feed *into* the workshop (recruiter brings collected stories and friction points), not replace it.

## Synthesis

The research yields **three core behavioral moves** a recruiter-intake agent should make on every turn to serve both discovery and sales-ammunition goals:

### Move 1: Ask for concrete past examples, not abstract requirements

Hiring managers default to generic requirements ("we need a strong communicator") and aspirational descriptions ("we have a collaborative culture"). These are low-signal for discovery and unusable for sales pitches. The agent must redirect every abstract claim to a concrete past example: "Tell me about the last person who succeeded in this role—what did they do?" "Walk me through the last project this team shipped." "Give me an example of a decision this person would make solo." Past examples reveal actual success criteria (discovery) and provide stories the recruiter can retell verbatim (sales).

### Move 2: Surface friction explicitly via transparent RJP probes

The Earnest et al. (2011) meta-analysis is unambiguous: honesty builds trust and reduces turnover. The agent must ask at least one friction probe per intake: "What was harder than expected on the last project?" "Why did the last person leave?" "What's one thing about this role that might surprise a candidate?" Hiring managers who answer candidly give the recruiter RJP ammunition ("yes, there's on-call, here's how we handle it"). Hiring managers who deflect or sugarcoat signal either culture problems or lack of self-awareness—both are yellow flags. The agent should not accept "everything is great" as an answer.

### Move 3: Extract across all five Gartner EVP categories, not just Rewards

Most intake calls overweight Rewards (compensation, title) and underweight Opportunity (growth), People (team), Work (autonomy), and Organisation (culture, mission). The agent must explicitly probe each category:

- **Rewards:** "What's the comp range and equity?"
- **Opportunity:** "What does growth look like here—give me an example of someone who grew?"
- **Work:** "How much ownership will this person have over decisions?"
- **People:** "Who will they work with? Tell me about one of them."
- **Organisation:** "Why does this work matter? Who benefits when this team succeeds?"

Each probe yields both discovery (requirements, team structure, autonomy level) and sales ammunition (comp transparency, growth story, mission narrative). A complete intake should touch all five categories and yield 3–5 concrete stories the recruiter can pitch.

## Gaps

### What EVP literature doesn't answer:

1. **No large-N empirical studies of intake-based EVP extraction effectiveness.** The research compares formal EVP workshops to no EVP, not workshops vs. intake shortcuts. We don't know whether recruiter-extracted EVP (single-source, 60-min call) produces better hiring outcomes than generic job descriptions.

2. **No controlled studies of dual-purpose probe effectiveness.** The claim that asking for past examples yields better discovery *and* sales ammunition is practitioner consensus, not experimental evidence. A/B testing recruiter intake methods would strengthen this.

3. **Limited evidence on small-company EVP methods.** The Daggerfinn and Entrepreneur guides are tactical blogs, not peer-reviewed research. Empirical studies of <50-employee EVP extraction are absent from the literature.

4. **No research on EVP coherence across multiple hiring managers.** When 10 hiring managers in the same company give 10 different EVP narratives during intake, what does the recruiter do? The literature doesn't address aggregation or conflict resolution.

5. **Insufficient evidence on role EVP vs. company EVP trade-offs by candidate segment.** The claim that senior hires prioritize role EVP and junior hires prioritize company EVP is logical but not empirically validated across industries/geographies.

### Hooks for R4-jtbd and Lead synthesis:

- **R4-jtbd** will cover Jobs-to-be-Done and narrative transportation frameworks. The EVP extraction probes here (especially "why does this work matter?") are JTBD-adjacent but don't fully unpack the "job the candidate is hiring this role to do." R4 should provide complementary probe design patterns.

- **Lead synthesis** must resolve the aggregation problem: how does the agent synthesize EVP across multiple intakes? Does it maintain per-role EVP or attempt company-wide EVP? If the latter, how does it handle contradictions?

- **Lead must decide: is the agent a single-call EVP extractor or a multi-call EVP synthesizer?** The evidence supports single-call extraction (intake yields 3–5 stories per call, sufficient for that role's pitch). Multi-call synthesis (aggregate across 10 hiring managers to produce company EVP) is a different design with different prompts.

- **Legal/compliance constraint (out of scope for R3, but flagged for Lead):** Some jurisdictions restrict salary range disclosure timing. The probe "What's the comp range?" may conflict with pay transparency laws (e.g., must disclose in posting vs. cannot disclose until offer). Lead must route around this.

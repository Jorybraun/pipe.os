## Findings by sub-question

### Q1 — Retained executive search intake

Retained executive search firms operate under an exclusive engagement model with structured contracts and payment milestones tied to deliverables [S4, S7]. The Association of Executive Search Consultants (AESC), which released updated professional standards in June 2024, requires member firms to develop a "comprehensive needs-assessment, position description, and search strategy including which industries and companies to target, cultural and diversity considerations, skills and experience, thought and managerial leadership" before beginning search work [S1, S2, S3]. This is not a checklist exercise but a strategic consulting engagement that positions the search consultant as an objective advisor operating under five foundational principles: ethics and integrity, excellence (focusing on "clients' unique business needs" using "rigorous, evidence-informed, results-focused methodologies"), objectivity, opportunity and inclusion, and confidentiality [S2].

The primary output artifact of retained search intake is the Position Specification, a multi-page document that details core competencies, preferred experience, soft skills, reporting relationships, responsibilities, and objectives [S4, S5, S6]. This document serves as a "touchstone" that prevents searches from veering off course and functions as a recruitment tool compelling enough to make a passive executive want a conversation [S7]. The position specification is not merely descriptive; it is strategic, building "a picture of the business context, the leadership gap, the stakeholder dynamics, and what success looks like at 12 months" [S7]. The intake process that produces this artifact is more thorough than most firms deliver, grounding the specification in the organizational reality rather than an idealized wish list.

Retained search intake meetings are typically longer than contingency or in-house TA intake calls, often extending to 90 minutes or more, given the depth of exploration required for executive-level roles. The intake covers not just the role itself but also stakeholder dynamics (who will resist this hire, who will champion it), cultural and diversity considerations, off-limits agreements (which companies or candidates are not to be approached), and the search strategy itself (which industries, competitors, or geographic regions will be targeted) [S1, S3, S7]. Payment structure spreads the cost across three milestones — one at engagement, one when the shortlist is presented, and one at placement — with each payment tied to a concrete deliverable, reinforcing the accountability of the search firm to produce documented work product at each stage [S7].

The exclusivity requirement is load-bearing in retained search. AESC members must have "an exclusive agreement for each assignment with the client," ensuring they have comprehensive understanding of the position and organizational culture before conducting searches [S2]. This exclusivity distinguishes retained search from contingency recruiting, where multiple firms compete simultaneously for the same placement, and it enables the depth of intake investment that produces the position specification artifact. The retained model treats intake as a billable consulting engagement, not a free sales conversation.

### Q2 — Contingency tech recruiting intake

Contingency tech recruiting operates under a fundamentally different economic model than retained search, and the intake process reflects this difference. Contingency firms (Robert Half, Insight Global, TEKsystems, Randstad, Hays Technology) market aggressive turnaround speeds: Robert Half claims to present qualified candidates within 48 hours, while some firms advertise two-hour intake-to-submission timelines [S11, S12]. This speed is not achieved through faster intake calls but through pre-built talent pools, structured delivery models, and templatized intake processes that map client requirements to pre-indexed candidate databases.

The contingency intake call is compressed but still requires role clarity. Unlike retained search, which invests in a multi-page position specification, contingency firms use standardized intake forms designed for rapid completion, often 15-30 minutes rather than 60-90 minutes [S11, S12]. The questions focus on immediate must-haves (specific technologies, years of experience, certifications, availability, rate/salary range) rather than strategic context or organizational culture. The intake form is optimized for keyword matching and Boolean search queries, not for building a narrative portrait of the ideal candidate.

High-volume contingency recruiting relies on scale and infrastructure that in-house TA teams and boutique retained firms lack. Artech, for example, onboards 900+ contractors per month with 650+ recruiters across three global recruiting centers [S11]. This volume is enabled by structured delivery models where the intake call follows a script, the intake form maps to standardized fields in an ATS, and the initial candidate submission happens within hours because the recruiter is searching a pre-populated database rather than building a target list from scratch. The trade-off is depth for speed: contingency recruiters gather enough information to identify plausible matches quickly, but they do not invest in the organizational context, stakeholder mapping, or success-metric definition that retained search and strategic in-house TA prioritize.

Contingency intake also differs in accountability structure. Because multiple firms may be working on the same requisition simultaneously, the intake call is not a collaborative strategy session but a race to submit candidates first. The hiring manager may give the same intake information to three or four contingency firms, and the firm that sources and submits a viable candidate first wins the placement fee. This creates an incentive for speed over thoroughness, and it positions the intake call as a transactional information exchange rather than a consultative partnership. The contingency recruiter is not designing a search strategy with the client; they are gathering enough data to execute a search strategy they already know how to run.

Despite the compressed timeline, professional contingency recruiters still distinguish must-haves from nice-to-haves, clarify dealbreakers (Will you accept candidates without a specific certification? Is remote work negotiable? What's the hard ceiling on rate?), and confirm the hiring process (How many interview rounds? Who makes the final decision? What's the target start date?). The best contingency recruiters bring market data to the intake call — current rate ranges for the skill set, estimated time-to-fill based on talent pool size, and competitor intelligence on what similar roles are offering — positioning themselves as experts even within a compressed timeframe [S11, S12]. But the core difference remains: contingency intake optimizes for rapid candidate submission, while retained search and strategic in-house TA optimize for alignment and long-term hiring quality.

### Q3 — In-house TA kickoff calls

Modern in-house talent acquisition teams at high-performing tech companies have professionalized the intake process, drawing on structured hiring methodologies from Greenhouse, Ashby, and Lever, academic research on interviewer reliability and bias, and thought leadership from practitioners like Lou Adler, Josh Bersin, and LinkedIn's Brendan Browne. The in-house TA kickoff call (also called an intake meeting, strategy meeting, or role alignment session) is positioned as the foundation of a structured hiring process, not an administrative formality [S13, S14, S15, S16, S17, S18, S19].

Greenhouse's structured hiring methodology, widely adopted across tech TA teams, specifies that the role kickoff meeting is "roughly an hour long" and produces three concrete outputs: (1) a Business Objectives Document defining goals at 90 days and one year, (2) a Skills/Traits/Qualifications Matrix mapping required abilities, personality characteristics, and experience credentials, and (3) a RACI assignment dividing responsibilities between the recruiter (scorecard building, interview design) and the hiring manager (technical content, subject matter expertise) [S15]. This is not a passive note-taking session; it is a co-design workshop where the recruiter and hiring manager build the hiring framework together. Greenhouse's three guiding questions anchor the conversation in outcomes rather than credentials: (1) "What goals need to be achieved a year from now for you to determine that the hire is successful?" (2) "What will a superstar hire accomplish in a year?" (3) "For a superstar to accomplish that, what do they need to do in the first 90 days?" [S15]. This outcome-backward structure forces hiring managers to articulate success metrics before discussing qualifications, preventing the default pattern of listing credentials without connecting them to business impact.

LinkedIn's Head of Recruiting, Brendan Browne, published a five-phase intake methodology in 2019 that remains canonical in modern TA practice [S18]. The five phases are: (1) Clarify role and business need (ask about background, team growth alignment, example candidate profiles, ensure stakeholders understand why the role is opening), (2) Define must-haves vs. nice-to-haves (Browne emphasizes: "The line between must-haves and nice-to-haves gets blurred when you don't drive clarity"), (3) Assess interviewing capabilities (confirm whether the team needs training in compliance, unconscious bias, inclusive hiring, or GDPR; "If people haven't been through interviewing training, they sure as heck should go through it"), (4) Set partnership expectations (discuss priorities, target timelines, preferred communication methods, interviewing approaches), and (5) Plan follow-up cadence (send recap email, schedule a follow-up meeting one week later, establish weekly or bi-weekly check-ins to discuss progress and sourcing strategy adjustments) [S18]. This framework positions the intake meeting as the start of a recurring partnership, not a one-time handoff.

LinkedIn's earlier 2016 research established that the intake form should cover three core areas: (1) what the hiring manager is looking for in the role, (2) expected timeline of the hire and expectations for both hiring manager and recruiter, and (3) mutual accountability standards where recruiters commit to "clear communication, high-quality candidates and adhering to a reasonable timeline," while hiring managers pledge to respond within one business day, conduct timely interviews, and immediately notify of changes [S19]. This mutual-commitment structure transforms intake from a unidirectional information flow into a two-way contract, establishing the recruiter and hiring manager as equals rather than hierarchical partners — a characteristic that Bersin by Deloitte research identified as defining exceptional recruiters [S19].

Lou Adler's Performance-based Hiring methodology, influential in Silicon Valley TA practice since the 1990s and codified in his book *Hire With Your Head*, replaces skills-based job descriptions with "performance profiles" containing 5-6 performance objectives that describe the task, the action required to complete it, and some measure of success, using action verbs like "build," "create," "upgrade," or "maintain" [S8, S9, S10]. Adler's intake methodology uses five key questions: (1) What's the major objective or biggest challenge? (2) What do the best people do differently than average people in this role? (3) Are there any team or managerial issues that need to be addressed? (4) How would you describe the environment this person will be working in? (5) Who would you rather hire, someone who can deliver the results, or someone who has all the skills? [S9]. The final question is a forcing function that surfaces whether the hiring manager is truly committed to outcomes-based hiring or will revert to credential screening. Adler's methodology also emphasizes the Employee Value Proposition (EVP), asking hiring managers to define "what's in it for the candidate" by clarifying why a top person would take this job over competing opportunities [S9]. This EVP becomes part of the performance profile and is used in candidate outreach, not just internal documentation.

John Vlastelica of Recruiting Toolbox, who has trained TA teams at Google, Slack, Disney, Bloomberg, and Target, challenges the term "intake form" itself, arguing that it frames recruiters as order-takers rather than strategic partners [S20]. He advocates calling it a "strategy meeting or kickoff conversation" and arriving with "research, recommendations, and clarifying questions" based on historical hiring patterns (sources of hire, time-to-fill, target candidate profiles) rather than a blank form [S20]. Vlastelica's preparation standard is that recruiters should know typical source-of-hire data, time-to-fill benchmarks, and target candidate profile information specific to the job category before the intake call, so they can lead the conversation with data-driven recommendations rather than asking "where do you think we'd find this talent?" without doing homework first [S20]. This repositions the intake call as a consulting engagement where the recruiter brings market intelligence and sourcing strategy, not just a notebook.

Google's structured interviewing methodology, documented in their re:Work resources and widely studied by TA practitioners, uses the intake process to define vetted high-quality questions, standardized rubrics, and interviewer training/calibration frameworks [S51, S52]. The intake call outputs the assessment framework upfront — specific interview questions tied to role requirements, scoring rubrics with anchored rating scales, and a RACI for who assesses which competencies — so that the interview process is designed before the first candidate is sourced. Google's research found that using pre-made questions, guides, and rubrics saves an average of 40 minutes per interview and that interviewers conducting structured interviews reported feeling more prepared [S52]. The intake investment pays dividends in downstream efficiency.

Netflix and Airbnb, both known for culture-first hiring, embed cultural alignment into the intake process. Netflix's VP of Talent Acquisition, Valarie Toda, advises candidates that "the No. 1 thing is read the culture memo," and the TA team sends Netflix's core values to candidates before the screening stage [S53, S54]. This means the intake call must explicitly define how the role connects to Netflix's "Dream Team" philosophy and which cultural values are most critical for success in the specific team context. Airbnb's hiring rubric makes culture fit a mandatory gatekeeper: a 'No Hire' decision in the Core Values interview cannot be overridden by strong technical performance [S55]. This non-negotiable veto means the intake call must define cultural fit criteria with the same rigor as technical requirements, not treat culture as a vague "we'll know it when we see it" dimension.

Josh Bersin's 2024 research on the future of talent acquisition documents a sector-wide shift toward the "talent advisor" model, where TA professionals are elevated from process managers to strategic consultants [S26, S27, S28]. Companies leading this transformation (Mastercard, for example, renamed their recruiters "Career Coaches") position intake calls as advisory sessions where recruiters bring market intelligence, sourcing strategy, and process design expertise, not just coordination services [S26]. Bersin's data shows that AI is delivering 75% efficiency gains in recruitment administration (resume screening, interview scheduling) and 50% improvements in sourcing speed (candidate identification, contact enrichment), freeing TA professionals to focus on strategic work like intake consultation, candidate experience design, and hiring manager education [S28]. The intake call is the primary vehicle for this strategic value delivery.

### Q4 — Standard artifacts and templates

The intake process produces multiple artifacts depending on the engagement model and organizational context. This section maps the common output artifacts across retained search, contingency recruiting, and in-house TA, identifying who produces each, who consumes it, and how they differ.

| Artifact | Producer | Consumer | Typical contents | Usage context |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Position Specification** | Retained search consultant | Client hiring team, search firm research team, prospective candidates | Multi-page document: role title, reporting relationships, responsibilities, organizational context, leadership gap being filled, core competencies, preferred experience, soft skills, success metrics at 12 months, compensation range, cultural/diversity considerations [S4, S5, S6, S7] | Retained executive search; serves as a "touchstone" preventing scope drift and as a candidate recruitment tool |
| **Search Strategy Document** | Retained search consultant | Client hiring team, search firm internal team | Target industries, companies, sourcing approach, diversity goals, timeline with reporting milestones, off-limits agreements [S7] | Retained search deliverable showing *how* the search will be conducted, distinct from the position specification showing *what* is being sought |
| **Performance Profile** | In-house TA or agency recruiter (using Lou Adler methodology) | Hiring manager, interview panel, candidates | 5-6 performance objectives (task + action + measure of success), Employee Value Proposition (EVP), environmental context, team/managerial considerations [S8, S9, S10] | Performance-based hiring organizations; replaces traditional job description |
| **Business Objectives Document** | In-house TA recruiter + hiring manager (co-created) | Hiring manager, interview panel, recruiter | Goals defining success at 90 days and one year, "superstar" performance benchmarks [S15] | Greenhouse structured hiring methodology; output of the kickoff meeting |
| **Skills/Traits/Qualifications Matrix** | In-house TA recruiter + hiring manager (co-created) | Hiring manager, interview panel, sourcing team | Mapping table: required abilities, personality characteristics, experience credentials, must-haves vs. nice-to-haves [S15] | Greenhouse structured hiring methodology; distinguishes capabilities from credentials |
| **RACI Assignment** | In-house TA recruiter + hiring manager (co-created) | Recruiter, hiring manager, interview panelists | Division of hiring responsibilities (recruiter: scorecard building, interview design; hiring manager: technical content, SME input) [S15] | Greenhouse structured hiring; clarifies who owns what in the hiring process |
| **Ideal Candidate Profile (ICP)** | In-house TA recruiter or sourcing specialist | Sourcing team, hiring manager | Specifics for the role, key culture drivers, job description, separation between skills (trainable expertise) and talents (inherent abilities), 30/60/90-day success milestones [S29, S30] | Sourcing and screening alignment; more detailed than a job posting, less formal than a performance profile |
| **Role Scorecard / Hiring Rubric** | In-house TA recruiter + hiring manager | Sourcing team, initial screeners | Framework defining what a "qualified" candidate looks like before sourcing begins, outlining must-have, nice-to-have, and bonus qualifications [S31] | Sourcing and screening tool; used *before* interviews to guide candidate identification |
| **Interview Scorecard** | In-house TA recruiter + hiring manager | Interview panelists, hiring committee | Assessment tool used *after* candidates reach the interview stage, listing criteria on the left (key skills, competencies, qualifications) and rating space on the right (interviewer assesses how well the candidate demonstrates each criterion) [S31] | Interview assessment; distinct from role scorecard |
| **Intake Form / Intake Summary** | In-house TA recruiter or contingency recruiter | Hiring manager, sourcing team, future recruiters working on similar roles | 12-category structured form: role basics, business context, responsibilities, success metrics (30/60/90/360 days), must-have skills, nice-to-have skills, soft skills/culture fit, compensation, sourcing guidance, interview process, dealbreakers, hiring manager commitments [S19, S40, S58, S59, S60] | Operational tool capturing structured data from the intake call; sent as recap email within 24 hours |
| **Intake Meeting Recap Email** | Recruiter | Hiring manager, interview panelists, recruiting team | Summary of key takeaways, agreed requirements, timelines, mutual commitments (recruiter commits to X, hiring manager commits to Y), next steps [S18, S40] | Post-intake accountability artifact; documents verbal agreements in writing |
| **Target Candidate Profile** | Recruiter or sourcing specialist | Sourcing team, sometimes shared with hiring manager | Demographic/psychographic profile of the ideal candidate: industries they come from, companies they work at, titles they hold, skills they have, motivations they respond to [S29, S58] | Sourcing playbook; may include example LinkedIn profiles or "look-alike" candidates |

The key distinction between these artifacts is **timing and audience**. The Position Specification (retained search) and Performance Profile (Lou Adler methodology) are narrative documents designed to be read by prospective candidates; they function as recruitment tools, not just internal alignment documents. The Business Objectives Document, Skills/Traits/Qualifications Matrix, and RACI Assignment (Greenhouse methodology) are internal co-creation artifacts produced during the intake meeting itself, designed to align the recruiter and hiring manager before sourcing begins. The Role Scorecard / Hiring Rubric is a sourcing and screening tool used before interviews, while the Interview Scorecard is an assessment tool used during interviews; they serve different stages of the funnel and should not be conflated [S31]. The Intake Form / Intake Summary is the operational backbone that captures structured data for reporting, analytics, and handoffs to future recruiters working on similar roles.

Not all organizations produce all artifacts. Contingency recruiting typically produces only an Intake Form and perhaps a Target Candidate Profile, skipping the narrative documents (Position Specification, Performance Profile) because the engagement model does not support the time investment. In-house TA teams at high-maturity organizations (Google, Netflix, Stripe, companies using Greenhouse or Lever) produce multiple artifacts because they treat intake as a strategic investment in hiring quality and team alignment. Retained search produces the most comprehensive artifact set because the economic model (exclusive engagement, structured fees, executive-level placements) justifies the documentation overhead.

### Q5 — Failure modes

The practitioner literature documents recurring failure modes when intake is done poorly, with measurable consequences for hiring speed, quality, and stakeholder alignment. These failure modes are not hypothetical; they are grounded in Gartner research, LinkedIn survey data, and case examples from TA practitioners.

**Skipping the intake meeting entirely** is the most fundamental failure mode. Recruiters who rely on a forwarded job spec without a live conversation with the hiring manager operate in a vacuum, unable to distinguish must-haves from nice-to-haves, understand business context, or clarify dealbreakers [S22, S23]. This leads to misaligned candidate submissions, hiring manager frustration ("These candidates don't understand what we need"), and wasted sourcing effort. Even experienced recruiters make this mistake when they assume a well-written job description is sufficient, but job descriptions describe responsibilities, not success metrics, and they rarely capture the "why now" or the hiring manager's implicit preferences.

**Failure to distinguish must-haves from nice-to-haves** creates "purple squirrel" and "purple unicorn" requisitions — searches for mythical perfect candidates who meet 100% of requirements [S48, S49, S50]. Job descriptions often demand conflicting requirements (10 years of experience with a 5-year-old technology, expert-level skills across multiple unrelated domains) or combine 2-3 different roles into one position because of budget constraints [S48, S50]. Brendan Browne's research emphasizes that "the line between must-haves and nice-to-haves gets blurred when you don't drive clarity," and failing to force prioritization leads to hiring managers rejecting qualified candidates for minor or irrelevant criteria [S18]. The consequence is a dramatically reduced candidate pool — potentially from hundreds to zero [S50] — and prolonged time-to-fill as recruiters search for candidates who do not exist at the offered compensation level.

**Champagne taste on a beer budget** is a named failure mode where hiring managers seek highly qualified candidates with compensation budgets that don't align with the market [S47]. PayScale research shows this happens when managers define jobs based on minimum requirements instead of full proficiency: candidates aren't going to settle for the minimum salary if the job description demands full-proficiency skills [S47]. The intake call should surface this misalignment early by bringing market data (salary ranges for the skill set, competitor benchmarks) and helping the hiring manager either adjust the budget or adjust the requirements. When this doesn't happen, recruiters waste weeks sourcing candidates who won't accept the offer, or they submit underqualified candidates who fit the budget but not the expectations, leading to mutual frustration.

**Misalignment on timeline and talent pool size** causes downstream requisition changes and delays. Gartner research found that when hiring managers aren't aligned on role requirements, organizations are 41% more likely to change requisitions mid-search, which leads to a 38% increase in time-to-fill, potentially adding an extra month or more to every open role [S40, S41]. Stakeholders often underestimate how long it takes to fill a role, overestimate the available talent pool, or set salary expectations that don't align with the market [S33, S34]. Without resetting these expectations upfront in the intake call — using data on historical time-to-fill, outreach-to-hire conversion ratios, and current talent supply/demand — recruiters face mid-search pivots ("Actually, we need someone with X skill instead of Y," "Can you find someone who will start two weeks earlier?") that invalidate prior sourcing work.

**Starting with the wrong questions** is a marker of junior recruiter behavior. Starting the intake call with "How many years of experience should the candidate have?" or "What title should they currently hold?" focuses on credentials rather than performance [S21, S22]. Expert recruiters ask "What do the best people do differently than average people in this role?" or "What would success look like for this person six months in?" [S9, S18, S38, S40]. The credential-first approach produces candidates who look good on paper but may not perform, while the performance-first approach surfaces candidates with non-traditional backgrounds who can deliver results. Lou Adler's fifth intake question — "Who would you rather hire, someone who can deliver the results, or someone who has all the skills?" — is designed to reveal whether the hiring manager is genuinely committed to outcomes-based hiring or will revert to credential screening when candidates arrive [S9].

**Inadequate documentation and follow-up** leads to alignment drift over time. Verbal-only intake calls, without a written summary sent within 24 hours, allow hiring managers and recruiters to remember different versions of the conversation [S18, S34, S40]. Best practice is to send an intake summary email recapping key takeaways, agreed requirements, timelines, and mutual commitments (recruiter commits to X, hiring manager commits to Y), and to schedule a follow-up meeting one week later to discuss sourcing strategy and early candidate feedback [S18, S40]. Without this discipline, the hiring manager's mental model of the ideal candidate drifts, and by the time the recruiter submits candidates, the hiring manager has changed their mind about what's important.

**Skipping compensation alignment** in the intake call defers a critical conversation to the offer stage, where it's too late [S33, S34]. Recruiters should capture salary range, bonus structure, equity options, and standout benefits during intake, and use market data to surface budget mismatches immediately ("The market rate for this skill set in this location is $X-Y, and you've budgeted $Z. Let's discuss how to close that gap or adjust the requirements."). When compensation is treated as a "we'll figure it out later" topic, recruiters source candidates who won't accept the eventual offer, wasting everyone's time.

**Failure to understand "why now"** results in misunderstanding business context and urgency [S35, S36, S37]. The intake question "Why is this role open now?" reveals whether the search is driven by growth, backfill, restructuring, or crisis, and it surfaces team dynamics and stakeholder politics [S35, S38]. Without this context, recruiters can't assess whether the role will actually solve the business problem it's intended to solve, or whether the hiring manager is looking for something else (a scapegoat for team dysfunction, a replacement for a function that should be redesigned, a band-aid for a systemic resourcing problem). The "why now?" question also uncovers whether urgency is genuine or manufactured; if the organization has survived without this hire for months, it can usually survive another 30 days of listening before acting [S35].

**Relying too heavily on nice-to-have skills** excludes qualified applicants who lack them [S22]. This is related to the must-have/nice-to-have blurring but manifests differently: hiring managers treat nice-to-haves as filters rather than tie-breakers, rejecting candidates who have all the must-haves but are missing one nice-to-have. The intake call should establish that nice-to-haves are used only when comparing multiple qualified candidates, not as knockout criteria.

**No upfront time commitment from the hiring manager** leads to candidates losing interest when the hiring manager takes forever to respond to submissions or schedule interviews [S22]. The intake call should extract explicit commitments: "You'll provide feedback on candidate submissions within 24 hours," "You'll hold interview slots on Tuesdays and Thursdays," "You'll make a decision within 48 hours of final interviews." Without these commitments, the hiring process stalls, and top candidates accept competing offers.

### Q6 — Canonical 60-minute timeline

The practitioner literature converges on a 30-60 minute duration for in-house TA intake meetings, with 45-60 minutes considered optimal [S32, S33, S34, S35]. Retained search intake may extend to 90 minutes given the depth of position specification work required for executive placements. Contingency recruiting compresses intake to 15-30 minutes to optimize for speed. The composite timeline below is derived from Noota.io, Recruitee, HireTruffle, LinkedIn, Greenhouse, and Metaview sources [S32, S33, S34, S40] and represents a consensus structure for in-house TA kickoff calls. Where sources disagree, the disagreement is noted.

**Minutes 0-5: Welcome & Goal Setting**

Open with introductions (if first time working with this hiring manager), set the agenda, and confirm time availability. State the meeting purpose explicitly: "We're here to define the role, align on expectations, establish success metrics, and design the hiring process" [S32, S40]. Noota.io emphasizes that this phase "establishes meeting purpose and desired outcomes," priming everyone to stay focused rather than drift into tactical weeds [S32]. Some sources recommend sharing the agenda in advance (via calendar invite or email) so hiring managers arrive prepared rather than offering reactive responses [S32, S40].

**Minutes 5-20: Role Clarification & Business Context**

This phase explores why the role exists and what problem it solves. Key questions: "Why is this role open now?" (new position vs. backfill), "Why is this role important? What pain points or opportunities does it address?", "What's the business context — growth, restructuring, crisis?" [S35, S36, S38]. Greenhouse's methodology focuses this phase on long-term and big-picture results rather than daily tasks, asking "What goals need to be achieved a year from now for you to determine that the hire is successful?" and "What will a superstar hire accomplish in a year?" [S15]. Lou Adler's approach asks "What's the major objective or biggest challenge?" and "What do the best people do differently than average people in this role?" [S9]. The goal is to understand the strategic context before diving into job specifications. HireTruffle allocates 10 minutes to "Role Context & Challenges," while Noota.io allocates 10-15 minutes to "Role Clarification" [S34, S32]. The longer allocation (15-20 minutes) is appropriate for senior or complex roles where organizational politics and stakeholder dynamics matter.

**Minutes 20-40: Candidate Profile — Must-Haves, Nice-to-Haves, Success Metrics**

This is the longest phase of the intake meeting, consuming roughly one-third of the total time. The focus is on defining what the ideal candidate looks like and what they will accomplish. Key questions: "What are the top 3-5 day-to-day responsibilities?", "Which qualifications are non-negotiable vs. trainable on the job?", "What should ideal candidates have built, achieved, or proven?", "Describe your best past hire in a similar role — what made them successful?", "What should this person accomplish in their first 30, 60, and 90 days?" [S32, S33, S38, S39, S56, S57]. Brendan Browne's methodology emphasizes that "the line between must-haves and nice-to-haves gets blurred when you don't drive clarity," so this phase must force explicit prioritization [S18]. Noota.io allocates 15-20 minutes to "Candidate Profile," while Recruitee and Metaview suggest 15 minutes for defining requirements and another 5-10 minutes for success metrics [S32, S33, S40]. Greenhouse's three guiding questions serve as scaffolding: start with 1-year success, work backward to 90-day ramp [S15]. This phase also covers soft skills and cultural fit: "What soft skills complement the team?", "How does this role fit into the team structure?", "What's the team culture like?" [S38]. The output is the Skills/Traits/Qualifications Matrix (Greenhouse) or the performance objectives (Lou Adler) or the must-have/nice-to-have breakdown (LinkedIn/Lever).

**Minutes 40-50: Compensation, Logistics, & Hiring Process**

This phase addresses budget, timeline, and process design. Compensation questions: "What's the salary range, bonus structure, equity, and standout benefits?", "Is the budget aligned with market rates for this skill set?" [S33, S34, S40]. Logistics questions: "Where is the position located? Remote options?", "What's the ideal start date?", "Is relocation assistance or visa sponsorship available?" [S38, S39]. Hiring process questions: "How many interview stages?", "Who participates at each stage?", "How quickly can the team provide feedback?", "What's the decision-making process — unanimous vote, hiring manager final call, committee?", "Are there any technical assessments or work samples?" [S32, S38, S40]. Noota.io allocates 10 minutes to "Compensation & Logistics" and 15-20 minutes to "Hiring Process & Decision-Making," while HireTruffle compresses both into a combined 10 minutes [S32, S34]. The longer allocation is appropriate when designing a structured interview panel (à la Google or Greenhouse), where the intake call outputs specific interview questions, scoring rubrics, and a RACI for who assesses which competencies [S51, S52]. This phase should also establish hiring manager commitments: "You'll provide feedback within 24 hours," "You'll hold interview slots on [specific days]," "You'll make a decision within 48 hours of final interviews" [S22, S40].

**Minutes 50-60: Sourcing Strategy, Dealbreakers, & Next Steps**

This phase covers sourcing approach and establishes follow-up rhythm. Sourcing questions: "Where should we look for talent — specific industries, competitors, geographic regions?", "Are there diversity goals for this search?", "Whom do you know who might be a fit or could refer someone?" [S38]. Dealbreaker questions: "What are absolute disqualifiers?", "What common hiring challenges have you faced with this role before?" [S38]. Next steps: summarize key takeaways, confirm mutual commitments, establish the follow-up cadence (LinkedIn recommends scheduling a follow-up meeting one week later to discuss sourcing strategy, then weekly or bi-weekly check-ins thereafter [S18]). The recruiter commits to sending an intake summary email within 24 hours recapping agreed requirements, timelines, and next steps [S18, S40]. Noota.io allocates 5 minutes to "Closing & Next Steps" [S32].

**Disagreements and variations:**

Contingency recruiting compresses the entire timeline to 15-30 minutes by skipping business context, cultural fit, and sourcing strategy discussions, focusing only on must-haves, rate/salary, and submission deadlines [S11, S12]. Retained search extends the timeline to 90+ minutes by adding stakeholder mapping, off-limits discussions, and search strategy co-creation [S7]. Lou Adler's Performance-based Hiring adds an EVP discussion ("Why would a top person take this job over competing opportunities?") that doesn't appear in other frameworks [S9]. Google's structured interviewing methodology front-loads interview design into the intake call, adding 10-15 minutes to develop interview questions and scoring rubrics [S51, S52]. Netflix and Airbnb add explicit cultural values discussion, asking hiring managers to identify which of the company's core values are most critical for success in this specific role [S53, S54, S55].

The 60-minute structure is aspirational; less experienced hiring managers may require 75-90 minutes, while repeat collaborations with the same hiring manager on similar roles may compress to 30-40 minutes because context and process are already established.

### Q7 — Expert vs. junior probes

The practitioner literature identifies specific questions and behaviors that distinguish expert recruiters from junior recruiters during intake calls. These distinctions are grounded in sources from Lou Adler, John Vlastelica, LinkedIn's Brendan Browne, Gem, Qualigence, and SHRM research on the talent advisor model [S9, S20, S18, S38, S39, S43, S44, S45].

**Junior recruiters ask credential questions; expert recruiters ask performance questions.** Starting the intake call with "How many years of experience should the candidate have?" or "What title should they currently hold?" is flagged as a mistake [S21, S22]. These questions focus on pedigree rather than capability. Expert recruiters ask "What do the best people do differently than average people in this role?" (Lou Adler [S9]), "What would success look like for this person six months in?" (Metaview [S40]), or "What should ideal candidates have built, achieved, or proven?" (Gem [S38]). The credential question produces a list of qualifications to filter against; the performance question produces a behavioral profile to source toward.

**Junior recruiters accept job descriptions as given; expert recruiters challenge and refine them.** John Vlastelica's research shows that junior recruiters arrive at intake meetings with a blank form and ask "Where do you think we'd find this talent?" without doing research first [S20]. Expert recruiters arrive with market data (historical time-to-fill, typical sources of hire, salary benchmarks, candidate pool size estimates) and use that data to educate the hiring manager: "The market rate for this skill set is $X-Y, and you've budgeted $Z. Let's discuss how to close that gap or adjust the requirements" [S20, S40]. Vlastelica emphasizes that expert recruiters "lead the conversation with data-driven recommendations rather than asking questions without doing homework first" [S20]. This repositions the recruiter as a consultant bringing expertise, not a clerk taking dictation.

**Junior recruiters treat must-haves and nice-to-haves as hiring manager preferences; expert recruiters force prioritization.** Asking "Should we instantly dismiss individuals who lack X in their resumes?" is an expert probe that surfaces whether a supposed "must-have" is actually a dealbreaker or just a preference [S22]. Brendan Browne's research emphasizes that "the line between must-haves and nice-to-haves gets blurred when you don't drive clarity," and expert recruiters use follow-up questions to force explicit rank-ordering [S18]. A junior recruiter might document a 10-item requirements list without prioritization; an expert recruiter reduces it to 3-4 non-negotiables and 3-4 strong preferences, preventing purple squirrel searches.

**Junior recruiters ask about the role in isolation; expert recruiters probe organizational and team context.** Expert questions include: "How does this role fit into the team structure?" [S38], "How does it support organizational goals?" [S38], "What team obstacles will this person need to navigate?" [S39], "Are there any team or managerial issues that need to be addressed?" (Lou Adler [S9]), "Who would this person work with in their day-to-day, and what are those people like?" [S17]. These questions surface political dynamics, team dysfunction, or structural problems that might cause a new hire to fail even if they have the right skills. A junior recruiter might source a technically qualified candidate who walks into a toxic team and quits within six months; an expert recruiter surfaces those dynamics in intake and either addresses them with the hiring manager or adjusts the candidate profile to find someone resilient enough to handle the environment.

**Junior recruiters ask what the candidate should do; expert recruiters ask what the best candidates do differently.** Lou Adler's second intake question — "What do the best people do differently than average people in this role?" — is a canonical expert probe [S9]. This question anchors the profile in a real exemplar (the best person who previously held this role) rather than an abstract ideal. It forces the hiring manager to articulate what exceptional performance looks like, not just adequate performance. A related expert question from Gem's framework is "Who's the best person who held this role previously? What made them successful?" [S38]. This provides a concrete benchmark and often reveals implicit preferences the hiring manager hadn't articulated.

**Junior recruiters accept stated requirements; expert recruiters probe the "why" behind them.** Asking "Why is industry experience critical for this role?" or "Why does this person need to have managed a team of exactly 5-10 people?" surfaces whether a requirement is truly load-bearing or just a proxy for something else [S38]. Often, hiring managers list requirements without interrogating why they matter. For example, "Must have worked at a Series B startup" might actually mean "Must be comfortable with ambiguity and rapid change," in which case candidates from other high-growth or turnaround environments might also qualify. Expert recruiters unpack these proxies.

**Junior recruiters ask about the role; expert recruiters ask about the business problem.** "Why is this role open now?" is an expert probe that reveals urgency, business context, and stakeholder dynamics [S35, S38]. A junior recruiter might accept "We need a senior engineer" as sufficient; an expert recruiter probes: "What business problem are you trying to solve by hiring this person? What happens if we don't fill this role? What's changed in the business that makes this role necessary now?" These questions surface whether the role is strategic (enabling new product launch, entering new market) or reactive (backfilling attrition, band-aiding a broken process). The answer shapes sourcing strategy and candidate messaging.

**Junior recruiters ask for a list of skills; expert recruiters ask about outcomes and impact.** Qualigence's 27-question framework distinguishes between "basic questions" (title, location, salary) and "candidate-specific questions" (primary challenge in first 90 days, three SMART objectives for 6-12 months, performance measurement criteria) [S39]. The candidate-specific questions are expert-level because they probe impact and outcomes. Similarly, asking "What are the top three contributions this new hire will make to the company within their first 90 or 120 days?" [S38] forces the hiring manager to articulate business value, not just job duties.

**Junior recruiters skip EVP; expert recruiters probe candidate motivations.** Lou Adler's methodology includes the Employee Value Proposition question: "Why is this job exciting? What's in it for the candidate? Why would a top person take this job over competing opportunities?" [S9, S17]. This surfaces the "sell" angle that will be used in candidate outreach. Junior recruiters treat recruiting as one-way evaluation (does the candidate meet our requirements?); expert recruiters recognize it's a two-way match (does our opportunity meet the candidate's needs?). The EVP question also reveals whether the hiring manager has thought about candidate motivations at all, or is assuming people will just be grateful to work there.

**Junior recruiters accept the hiring manager's network as a black box; expert recruiters activate it as a sourcing channel.** Gem's framework includes "Whom do you know?" as an intake question [S38], turning the hiring manager into a sourcing partner. Expert recruiters ask "Who in your network might be a fit for this role, or could refer someone?" and use the intake call to seed a referral pipeline. Junior recruiters treat sourcing as their job alone; expert recruiters recognize that hiring managers often know or can be introduced to top talent, and activating that network is more efficient than cold outreach.

**Junior recruiters ask hiring managers to describe the ideal candidate; expert recruiters ask hiring managers to describe the best actual candidate.** "Describe your best past hire in a similar role — what made them successful?" [S32] is grounded in reality rather than aspiration. Hiring managers often idealize when asked to describe the "perfect" candidate, listing attributes no real human possesses. Asking about the best past hire anchors the conversation in an actual person, revealing what trade-offs the hiring manager made and which attributes mattered most in practice.

**For senior-level roles, expert recruiters probe leadership scope and trajectory.** Qualigence's research identifies specific probes for senior roles: "How many teams or people will they handle?" [S39], "What should the ideal candidate achieve in 30 days, 90 days, and 360 days?" [S39], "What will be the career development path for the ideal candidate in the organization in the next 3-5 years?" [S39]. Junior recruiters might use the same intake template for junior, mid, and senior roles; expert recruiters recognize that senior roles require additional depth on leadership trajectory, organizational impact, and multi-year vision.

**Expert recruiters also probe interviewer readiness, while junior recruiters assume the hiring team is prepared.** Brendan Browne's framework includes "Assess interviewing capabilities: confirm whether the team needs training in compliance, unconscious bias, inclusive hiring, or GDPR. 'If people haven't been through interviewing training, they sure as heck should go through it'" [S18]. This surfaces whether the hiring manager and interview panel are trained to conduct structured, legally compliant, bias-resistant interviews, or whether the recruiter needs to provide training before candidates enter the pipeline.

**A bonus expert probe for senior/specialized roles:** "Should we speak with someone who currently holds a similar role to understand nuances you might miss?" [S39]. This recognizes that hiring managers managing a role indirectly (e.g., a VP hiring a Senior Data Scientist when the VP's background is in operations, not data science) may not fully understand the day-to-day realities. Involving a current role occupant in the intake or interview design surfaces technical or cultural nuances the hiring manager can't articulate.

## Cross-tradition synthesis

Despite the differences between retained executive search, contingency tech recruiting, and in-house TA, the intake practice literature converges on seven universal elements every good intake call shares, regardless of engagement model or organizational context.

**First, intake is a strategic conversation, not a transactional form-fill.** John Vlastelica's critique of the term "intake form" [S20], the SHRM and SocialTalent research on the talent advisor model [S43, S44, S45], and Brendan Browne's emphasis on "establishing immediate trust with the hiring manager" [S18] all point to the same insight: intake is a consulting engagement where the recruiter brings expertise (market intelligence, sourcing strategy, process design) and the hiring manager brings business context and role requirements. The conversation is co-creative, not one-directional. Recruiters who position themselves as order-takers produce lower-quality hires and command less respect from hiring managers; recruiters who position themselves as strategic partners co-design the role and the hiring process.

**Second, intake must distinguish must-haves from nice-to-haves, and this distinction must be forced, not assumed.** This appears in every tradition: Brendan Browne (LinkedIn) emphasizes it as a core phase of the intake checklist [S18], Greenhouse's methodology builds it into the Skills/Traits/Qualifications Matrix [S15], Lou Adler's Performance-based Hiring forces it through the "Who would you rather hire, someone who can deliver the results or someone who has all the skills?" question [S9], and the purple squirrel literature documents the cost of failing to do it [S48, S49, S50]. Without explicit prioritization, hiring managers treat everything as non-negotiable, producing searches for candidates who don't exist or don't exist at the offered compensation.

**Third, intake must capture outcomes and success metrics, not just responsibilities and credentials.** Lou Adler's performance objectives [S9], Greenhouse's three guiding questions (1-year success → 90-day ramp) [S15], the 30-60-90 day milestone framework [S56, S57], and the "What do the best people do differently?" probe [S9] all operationalize the same principle: the intake call should define what this person will accomplish, not just what they will do. Job descriptions list duties; performance profiles define impact. The shift from duty-based to outcome-based intake is the hallmark of mature TA practice.

**Fourth, intake must produce artifacts, not just notes.** Retained search produces a Position Specification and Search Strategy document [S4, S5, S6, S7], Greenhouse produces a Business Objectives Document, Skills/Traits/Qualifications Matrix, and RACI assignment [S15], Lou Adler produces a Performance Profile [S9], and even fast contingency recruiting produces a structured Intake Form [S58, S59, S60]. Verbal-only intake leads to alignment drift; documented intake creates shared accountability. The artifact should be sent within 24 hours of the intake call and referenced throughout the hiring process to prevent scope creep [S18, S40].

**Fifth, intake must include compensation alignment and market data.** This appears consistently across LinkedIn's intake form methodology (one of the three core areas [S19]), the compensation/logistics phase of the composite timeline [S32, S33, S34], the "champagne taste on a beer budget" failure mode [S47], and the expert vs. junior recruiter distinction (experts bring market data to reset unrealistic expectations [S20, S40]). Deferring compensation discussion to the offer stage is a known failure mode that wastes sourcing effort and leads to declined offers.

**Sixth, intake is not a one-time meeting but the start of a recurring partnership.** LinkedIn's methodology specifies follow-up cadence as the fifth phase of the intake checklist: send a recap email, schedule a follow-up meeting one week later, establish weekly or bi-weekly check-ins to discuss progress and adjust strategy [S18]. SHRM research on recruiter-hiring manager partnership emphasizes ongoing communication and feedback loops [S41, S42]. Gartner data shows that misalignment during intake leads to mid-search requisition changes that add 30+ days to time-to-fill [S40, S41], and these changes happen when there's no recurring check-in rhythm to catch drift early.

**Seventh, expert intake probes the "why" behind the "what."** "Why is this role open now?" [S35, S38], "Why is industry experience critical?" [S38], "What business problem are you trying to solve?" [S35], and "Why would a top person take this job over competing opportunities?" [S9] all exemplify expert-level inquiry. Junior recruiters accept stated requirements at face value; expert recruiters interrogate the assumptions behind them. This probing surfaces hidden constraints (political dynamics, team dysfunction, budget mismatches), implicit preferences (the hiring manager wants someone "like Sarah" but hasn't articulated what made Sarah successful), and strategic context (this role is a precursor to a larger org redesign, this hire will be evaluated as a proxy for the hiring manager's judgment).

**Where the three traditions diverge:**

**Speed vs. depth.** Contingency recruiting optimizes for rapid candidate submission (24-48 hours [S11, S12]), compressing intake to 15-30 minutes and skipping business context, cultural fit, and sourcing strategy discussions. Retained search invests 90+ minutes in intake to produce a multi-page Position Specification because the engagement model (exclusive contract, structured fees) supports the time investment [S4, S7]. In-house TA falls in the middle: 45-60 minutes for most roles, extending to 90 minutes for senior/complex positions [S32, S33, S34, S35].

**Artifact formality.** Retained search produces client-ready narrative documents (Position Specification, Search Strategy) designed to be read by executive candidates and presented to search committees [S4, S7]. In-house TA produces structured internal documents (Business Objectives, Skills Matrix, RACI) designed for hiring team alignment [S15]. Contingency recruiting produces operational forms optimized for ATS integration and keyword search [S58, S60].

**Exclusivity and partnership model.** Retained search operates under exclusive agreements with mutual accountability [S2, S4], positioning the search firm as a strategic partner whose fees are tied to deliverables. Contingency recruiting often involves multiple firms competing simultaneously for the same placement, creating a race dynamic rather than a partnership [S11, S12]. In-house TA has an inherent partnership with hiring managers (they work for the same organization) but must earn strategic advisor status through competence and trust-building [S43, S44, S45].

**Compensation structure.** Retained search fees are paid in thirds (upon engagement, at shortlist, at placement [S7]), creating accountability at each stage. Contingency fees are paid only upon placement, creating a volume incentive (submit candidates fast, move to the next search) rather than a quality incentive. In-house TA recruiters are salaried employees whose performance is evaluated on hiring quality, time-to-fill, hiring manager satisfaction, and candidate experience, aligning incentives with long-term organizational health rather than individual placement volume.

**Sourcing approach.** Retained search builds a custom target list for each assignment, often conducting original research to identify passive candidates who aren't actively job-seeking [S4, S7]. Contingency recruiting sources from pre-built talent pools and ATS databases, optimizing for speed [S11, S12]. In-house TA blends both: sourcing from referral networks and warm pipelines for speed, supplemented by targeted outreach for hard-to-fill roles.

Despite these divergences, the seven universal elements hold: strategic conversation, must-have/nice-to-have distinction, outcome-based definition, artifact production, compensation alignment, recurring partnership, and "why" probing. An intake call that lacks any of these elements is deficient, regardless of engagement model.

## What PIPE's current agent is missing

Based on the research, PIPE's current Role Discovery agent is missing several concrete practices that distinguish expert intake from junior intake and that the literature identifies as load-bearing for hiring quality and alignment.

**The agent does not appear to force must-have vs. nice-to-have prioritization.** Brendan Browne's research emphasizes that "the line between must-haves and nice-to-haves gets blurred when you don't drive clarity" [S18], and the purple squirrel literature documents the cost of failing to do this [S48, S49, S50]. The agent should not accept a hiring manager's requirement list at face value; it should challenge: "You've listed 12 requirements. If you could only have 3-4 of these and had to compromise on the rest, which 3-4 are non-negotiable?" This is not optional; it's a core discipline that prevents searches for candidates who don't exist.

**The agent does not capture performance objectives or outcome-based success metrics.** Lou Adler's performance profile [S9], Greenhouse's three guiding questions [S15], and the 30-60-90 day milestone framework [S56, S57] all operationalize outcomes-based intake. The agent should ask "What will a superstar hire accomplish in their first year?" and "What do they need to do in the first 90 days to set themselves up for that success?" rather than just "What are the responsibilities?" The current agent appears to focus on skills and qualifications (the "what") rather than accomplishments and impact (the "why").

**The agent does not appear to probe "Why now?" or business context.** The intake question "Why is this role open now?" reveals urgency, team dynamics, and whether the search is driven by growth, backfill, or restructuring [S35, S38]. Without this, the agent can't assess whether the role will solve the actual business problem. The current agent may capture that a role is "Senior Frontend Engineer" but not whether it's senior because the team is scaling rapidly (need someone who can hire and mentor), because the last person left due to team dysfunction (need someone resilient to politics), or because the product is pivoting (need someone comfortable with ambiguity and rapid change). These contexts require different candidate profiles.

**The agent does not distinguish between skills (trainable) and talents (inherent).** The Ideal Candidate Profile research [S29, S30] and the SeekOut rubric vs. scorecard distinction [S31] both emphasize separating skills (expertise and experience that can be taught) from talents (inborn abilities and natural characteristics that are hard to train). The agent should ask "Which of these are skills we can train, and which are talents someone either has or doesn't?" This prevents over-indexing on credentials and opens sourcing to candidates with non-traditional backgrounds.

**The agent does not produce a formal artifact.** Greenhouse produces a Business Objectives Document, Skills/Traits/Qualifications Matrix, and RACI assignment [S15]. Lou Adler produces a Performance Profile [S9]. Retained search produces a Position Specification [S4, S7]. The current PIPE agent appears to store structured data (key-value fields in a database) but does not output a human-readable summary document that can be sent to the hiring manager for confirmation, shared with interview panelists, or used as a candidate recruitment tool. The research is clear: verbal-only intake leads to drift; documented intake creates accountability [S18, S40].

**The agent does not bring market data or use it as a forcing function for unrealistic expectations.** Expert recruiters arrive at intake calls with salary benchmarks, candidate pool size estimates, and time-to-fill data [S20, S40]. They use this data to reset expectations: "The market rate for this skill set is $X-Y, and you've budgeted $Z. Let's discuss how to close that gap or adjust the requirements." The current PIPE agent does not appear to have access to market intelligence (salary data, talent supply/demand, competitor benchmarking) or to surface mismatches during the intake conversation. This means "champagne taste on a beer budget" problems [S47] and "purple squirrel" searches [S48] are discovered late rather than prevented early.

**The agent does not probe the "why" behind requirements.** Expert questions like "Why is industry experience critical for this role?" or "What do the best people do differently than average people in this role?" [S9, S38] unpack proxies and surface implicit preferences. The current agent likely asks "What industry experience is required?" and accepts the answer without interrogation. This misses opportunities to broaden the candidate pool by identifying what the requirement is actually a proxy for.

**The agent does not appear to ask "Who's the best person who held this role previously?"** This is a canonical expert probe [S38] that anchors the profile in a real exemplar rather than an abstract ideal. The hiring manager's answer reveals what trade-offs they made in practice and which attributes mattered most. The current agent likely asks for an idealized candidate description, which produces less useful guidance.

**The agent does not probe the Employee Value Proposition (EVP).** Lou Adler's methodology asks "Why is this job exciting? What's in it for the candidate? Why would a top person take this job over competing opportunities?" [S9]. This surfaces the "sell" angle for candidate outreach and reveals whether the hiring manager has thought about candidate motivations. The current agent focuses on what the company needs from the candidate, not what the candidate will get from the company.

**The agent does not establish a follow-up rhythm or mutual commitments.** LinkedIn's methodology specifies that intake should end with a recap email and a scheduled follow-up meeting one week later, then weekly or bi-weekly check-ins [S18]. The current agent completes the intake interview and hands off to the next phase (challenge design), but it doesn't establish a recurring partnership cadence to catch drift or misalignment early.

**The agent does not assess interviewer readiness or offer to design the interview process.** Brendan Browne's framework includes "Assess interviewing capabilities: confirm whether the team needs training in compliance, unconscious bias, inclusive hiring" [S18]. Google's methodology designs interview questions and scoring rubrics during intake [S51, S52]. The current PIPE agent focuses on the Role Discovery phase but does not extend into interview design, leaving hiring managers to figure out how to assess candidates on their own. The research suggests that intake and interview design are coupled: the intake call should output not just a role definition but also an assessment framework (which competencies will be assessed, by whom, using what questions/exercises).

**The agent may not be capturing compensation details or using them to validate feasibility.** The research is unanimous that compensation alignment must happen in the intake call [S19, S33, S34], yet this is often uncomfortable for hiring managers and agents may avoid it. The current agent should explicitly ask for salary range, bonus structure, equity, and benefits, and it should flag mismatches ("The range you've provided is below market for this skill set; let's discuss how to adjust").

**The agent does not appear to challenge unrealistic timelines.** Gartner research shows that misalignment on timeline expectations leads to 38% increases in time-to-fill [S40, S41]. Expert recruiters bring historical time-to-fill data and outreach-to-hire conversion ratios to set realistic expectations. The current agent may ask "When do you want this person to start?" but probably doesn't push back with "Based on the requirements you've described and the current talent market, a realistic time-to-fill is X weeks. If you need someone sooner, we'll need to adjust the requirements or the compensation."

In summary, the current agent appears to conduct a **structured interview** (following a question script) but not a **strategic intake conversation** (challenging assumptions, forcing prioritization, bringing market data, probing the "why," producing artifacts, establishing partnerships). The gap is not in coverage (the agent may ask about skills, responsibilities, team, culture) but in **depth and forcing functions**. Expert intake is adversarial in the sense that it does not let hiring managers off the hook for vague or unrealistic requirements; it forces clarity, prioritization, and data-driven realism. The research suggests that PIPE's agent should be more confrontational (in a respectful, data-backed way) and more artifact-oriented (producing a formal document, not just storing fields).

## Gaps

Several dimensions of intake practice are underspecified in the research or fall outside the scope of this brief, leaving hooks for R3-evp and R4-jtbd to address.

**What makes a compelling Employee Value Proposition (EVP) is referenced but not fully operationalized.** Lou Adler asks "Why would a top person take this job over competing opportunities?" [S9], and the research on talent advisor positioning emphasizes that expert recruiters develop "sell sheets" showing what the team offers vs. competitors [S24], but the practitioner literature does not provide detailed EVP frameworks, templates, or taxonomies of what motivates top talent (career growth, learning opportunities, mission alignment, compensation, work-life balance, team quality, technical challenges, brand equity, autonomy). R3-evp is tasked with researching EVP creation and employer branding, and that work should define what a strong EVP contains and how to elicit it from hiring managers during intake.

**How to probe for cultural fit without introducing bias is acknowledged but not solved.** Netflix, Airbnb, and Greenhouse all emphasize cultural alignment [S53, S54, S55], and Brendan Browne's intake checklist includes "assess interviewing capabilities" with training on unconscious bias [S18], but the research does not provide concrete interview questions or rubrics that surface cultural fit without defaulting to "culture fit = people like us." The "what soft skills complement the team?" question [S38] is vague and susceptible to in-group bias. R4-jtbd may address this through Jobs-to-be-Done or narrative transportation frameworks that anchor culture questions in work context rather than social similarity.

**When to involve other stakeholders (current role occupants, skip-level managers, cross-functional partners) in the intake process is mentioned but not systematized.** Qualigence suggests a bonus probe: "Should we speak with someone who currently holds a similar role to understand nuances you might miss?" [S39], and Greenhouse's RACI assignment implies that interview panelists may be involved in intake [S15], but the research does not specify when multi-stakeholder intake is necessary vs. when a single hiring manager conversation suffices. For PIPE's multi-stakeholder discovery redesign (referenced in the memory context), this is a gap: how many people should be interviewed during Role Discovery, in what order, and how should their inputs be weighted when they conflict?

**How to handle intake when the role is brand new (no "best person who held this role previously" to reference) is not addressed.** Many of the expert probes assume there's a precedent: "Who's the best person who held this role previously?" [S38], "What do the best people do differently than average people in this role?" [S9], "Describe your best past hire in a similar role" [S32]. But for truly novel roles (first data scientist at a traditional company, first Head of AI Safety, first community manager for a new product), there is no internal exemplar. The research does not provide alternative frameworks for defining success in net-new roles.

**How to adapt intake for very junior roles (entry-level, internships) vs. very senior roles (C-suite, board positions) is underspecified.** Qualigence identifies some senior-specific probes (team size, career development path over 3-5 years [S39]), but the research does not provide a comprehensive mapping of how intake questions should vary by seniority level. For example, entry-level roles may prioritize learning opportunities and mentorship over strategic impact, while C-suite roles may require board alignment and investor relationship probes that don't appear in the standard intake frameworks.

**The relationship between intake and ongoing workforce planning / talent pipelining is acknowledged but not operationalized.** Dr. John Sullivan's research mentions using the hiring process to capture names of top performers for future talent pipelines [S24], and some sources reference intake as an input to workforce planning, but the research does not specify how intake data should feed multi-year hiring plans or how to structure intake when hiring for a pipeline (e.g., "We'll need 3-5 backend engineers over the next 12 months") rather than a single opening.

**How to handle confidential or politically sensitive searches is barely addressed.** Retained search mentions confidentiality as a core value [S2] and off-limits agreements [S3], but the research does not provide detailed guidance on conducting intake when the role is a stealth backfill (the current occupant doesn't know they're being replaced), a politically fraught hire (the team is divided on whether the role should exist), or a confidential executive search (the company doesn't want competitors to know they're hiring for this position). These scenarios require modified intake protocols that the general TA literature doesn't cover.

**The boundary between intake and challenge design for PIPE specifically is unclear.** PIPE's agent conducts Role Discovery and then transitions to challenge design (selecting or generating technical assessments, code review scenarios, behavioral interview questions). The research on intake focuses on defining the role and the ideal candidate, but it does not specify how that definition should map to assessment design. Google's structured interviewing methodology [S51, S52] comes closest by designing interview questions and rubrics during intake, but even that doesn't extend to work sample tests or multi-turn simulations like PIPE's code review arena. R4-jtbd may provide frameworks for translating role requirements into assessment scenarios, but the intake literature treats assessment design as a separate downstream activity.

These gaps are not failures of the research; they mark the edges of what the professional recruiter intake playbook covers. The next phase of research (R3-evp and R4-jtbd) will address several of these, and some (multi-stakeholder intake, confidential searches, assessment design) may require original synthesis beyond published practitioner material.

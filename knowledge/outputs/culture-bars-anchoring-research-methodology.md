# Culture BARS Anchoring — Methodology Research (R1)

## Summary

The foundational methodology for constructing Behaviorally Anchored Rating Scales (BARS) rests on two 1950s–60s techniques: Flanagan's (1954) Critical Incident Technique [S1] for generating the behavioral raw material, and Smith & Kendall's (1963) retranslation procedure [S2] for converting those incidents into defensible scale anchors. Together they form a 6-step protocol that any competent IO practitioner can follow for soft-skill dimensions — the technique was never restricted to clinical or technical domains.

The "checklists penalize experts, globals favour experts" finding from Hodges et al. (1999) [S3] and Hodges & McIlroy (2003) [S4] is fully replicable outside clinical OSCE in the following qualified sense: for *procedural* behavioral dimensions (specific steps performed in order), checklists are still appropriate; for *communicative and interpersonal* soft-skill dimensions — exactly the five behavioral competencies and five culture dimensions PIPE is targeting — anchored global ratings (BARS) are empirically superior to binary checklists. The mechanism is identical: expert performers integrate multiple sub-behaviors efficiently, scoring below novices on itemized checklists while scoring higher on overall impression. BARS anchors capture integrated patterns, not item counts.

The [UNSOURCED] Maurer 2002 claim from the parent brief is **fully resolved and accurate**. Maurer (2002) [S6] published "A practitioner-based analysis of interviewer job expertise and scale format as contextual factors in situational interviews" in *Personnel Psychology* (55, 307–327). Two police-officer samples (job-content experts) and a naive student sample were compared using either a BARS scale or a traditional format. Job experts produced no better ratings than naive raters when BARS were used — the scale format did the cognitive work that rater expertise normally provides. This is precisely the claim in the parent brief. The citation detail (BARS equalise rater quality) is supported.

## 1. The Smith & Kendall Retranslation Method

**Primary source:** Smith, P. C., & Kendall, L. M. (1963). Retranslation of expectations: An approach to the construction of unambiguous anchors for rating scales. *Journal of Applied Psychology, 47*(2), 149–155. [S2]

### What the method is

Smith & Kendall introduced the "behavioral expectations scale" (BES) as a way to build rating scales with *unambiguous* anchors [S2]. The core insight: evaluators produce different ratings not because they observe different behaviors but because they map observations onto anchor language differently. Making anchor language behaviorally concrete and retranslation-verified removes the mapping ambiguity.

### The six-step retranslation protocol (as documented in secondary literature citing the original)

1. **Job analysis / dimension identification.** Subject matter experts (SMEs) — typically 5–12 incumbents or supervisors — identify the performance dimensions of the job through structured workshops or job analysis interviews [S2][S7]. For soft-skill interview contexts, these dimensions are the behavioral competencies being assessed (e.g., ownership, collaboration, learning orientation).

2. **Critical incident collection (CIT pass).** A separate SME group generates specific behavioral incidents for each dimension — concrete accounts of observable actions that were notably effective or ineffective (see §2 for CIT detail). Smith & Kendall used head nurses; the technique is domain-agnostic [S1][S2].

3. **Incident sorting / first-pass retranslation.** A *third* SME group (not overlapping with groups 1 or 2) receives all incidents in randomised order, along with dimension definitions and titles only. Each incident is independently assigned to the dimension it best exemplifies. **Retention criterion:** an incident is kept only if ≥60–70% of sorters assign it to the same dimension as the generating group did [S7][S8]. This is the "retranslation" step — incidents that fail it are discarded as dimensionally ambiguous.

4. **Scaling.** Retained incidents are rated on a scale (typically 1–9 for the original method; 1–5 or 1–7 in interview BARS practice) for the degree to which the described behavior represents effective/ineffective performance. Agreement statistics (standard deviation of scale ratings) are computed. Incidents with high SD (raters disagree on their effectiveness level) are dropped [S2][S7].

5. **Anchor assignment.** Remaining incidents with low SD are positioned at scale points corresponding to their mean effectiveness rating. The result is a rating scale with observable-behavior anchors at each point, verified to be unambiguous about both (a) the dimension they belong to and (b) the level of performance they represent [S2].

6. **Final editing.** Language is standardized for clarity without changing meaning. Anchors that survive this process are the BARS product [S7][S8].

### What the original study showed

In Smith & Kendall's nurse study, retranslation agreement rates were high enough that scale reliability exceeded .97 [S2]. This very high reliability was attributed directly to the unambiguity procedure — raters were not applying personal interpretations of anchor language but matching observed behaviors to explicit descriptions.

### Why this matters for PIPE

The retranslation requirement is a quality gate, not a formality. For PIPE's culture BARS, applying step 3 (retranslation sorting) to any anchor bank generated by R2/R3 is the difference between "we wrote some behavioral descriptions" and "we have a dimensionally valid BARS". At minimum, 3–5 independent raters should sort the anchors before they are locked.

## 2. Critical Incident Technique for Soft Skills

**Primary source:** Flanagan, J. C. (1954). The critical incident technique. *Psychological Bulletin, 51*(4), 327–357. [S1]

### Definition

Flanagan defined a critical incident as "any observable human activity that is sufficiently complete in itself to permit inferences and predictions to be made about the person performing the act." Two conditions: (a) the observed behavior must have a clear *purpose or intent*, and (b) the *consequences* of the act must be sufficiently definite to leave little doubt about its effects [S1].

For interview BARS purposes, this definition maps neatly: an anchor is not "demonstrated ownership of the problem" (inferred trait) but "explicitly named themselves as responsible for the error and described specific actions they took to fix it before involving their manager" (observable act with visible consequence). The Flanagan definition operationally rules out inferential anchors.

### CIT collection procedure (Flanagan's five steps)

1. **Determine the objective.** Define the domain (e.g., "self-awareness during high-stakes decisions") and what counts as success or failure within it.
2. **Establish specifications.** Define who qualifies as an observer (SMEs with direct observation of the target behavior); set the situational frame (e.g., interviews, team retrospectives, code review discussions).
3. **Collect data.** Gather incidents through structured interviews, focus groups, written reports, or — as validated in 2017 by Kell et al. [S8] — crowdsourced online responses. For each incident: (a) describe the situation, (b) describe the person's specific actions, (c) describe the outcome.
4. **Analyze data.** Group incidents into performance categories (dimensions). Apply content analysis to derive dimension labels from the incident clusters. For interview BARS, this step confirms or extends the existing dimension list from the parent brief.
5. **Communicate conclusions.** Document the incident bank, the dimension structure, and the retranslation statistics [S1].

### Scale for soft skills specifically

For non-technical interpersonal competencies, Green, Alter, & Carr (1993) [S9] showed that valid anchors can be derived from written notes about candidate responses to similar past-behavior questions — an approach that sidesteps expensive workplace observation while maintaining behavioral specificity. Kell et al. (2017) [S8] replicated this with online crowdsourcing, generating 430 usable incidents from 68 respondents for 4 social-skill dimensions with acceptable inter-rater agreement. This is directly applicable to PIPE's context: candidates' interview transcripts from development/calibration runs can serve as the incident corpus.

### Minimum incident volume

No single threshold is established in the literature, but practice norms suggest 50–100 incidents per dimension before retranslation culling, expecting ~50% loss through the retranslation and SD filters [S7][S8]. For PIPE's 10 dimensions, this implies collecting ~500–1000 anchor candidates in total before producing a final 5-anchor-per-dimension BARS.

## 3. Hodges Findings — Do They Translate to Behavioral Interviews?

**Primary sources:** Hodges et al. (1999) [S3]; Hodges & McIlroy (2003) [S4]

### The clinical finding

Hodges, Regehr, McNaughton, Tiberius, & Hanson (1999) ran 14 clerks, 14 residents, and 14 experienced family physicians through two 15-minute standardized patient interviews, scored on both binary checklists and global process ratings. **Finding:** On global scales, experienced clinicians significantly outperformed both groups. On checklists, experienced clinicians scored significantly *worse* than residents and clerks [S3].

The paradox is mechanistically explained: expert clinicians integrate multiple sub-behaviors fluidly, often skipping redundant steps or performing them non-sequentially, which registers as omissions on a binary checklist. The same integration registers as sophisticated performance on a global dimension rating [S3].

Hodges & McIlroy (2003) extended this by developing an *analytic global* rating (sub-scaled into empathy, coherence, verbal communication, non-verbal expression) — not a checklist, not a single holistic impression. This "analytic global" achieved higher inter-station reliability (0.70) than checklists (0.54) and was sensitive to training level (Year 4 students significantly outperformed Year 3 on the summated scale) [S4].

### Translation to behavioral interviews

The translation is qualified but warranted:

**Where the Hodges finding applies directly:**
- Communication-quality dimensions (how clearly did the candidate structure their narrative, adapt to follow-ups, demonstrate self-awareness through language choices) — these are integration-dependent, exactly the Hodges case.
- Culture dimensions like "feedback orientation" or "autonomy preference" — these are expressed through integrated behavioral patterns, not discrete yes/no acts.

**Where the translation is imperfect:**
- OSCE assesses procedural medical skills (history-taking, examination). PIPE's dimensions are all interpersonal/attitudinal. The original paradox arose because experts *are* more efficient procedurally. In behavioral interviews, "efficiency" is not a confound — candidates are expected to narrate, not perform clinical tasks.
- PIPE's BARS anchors are globally-rated by definition (5-point scale with behavioral descriptions, not binary). The risk is the *opposite* of Hodges' case: binary probes or yes/no scoring rubrics would create the Hodges failure mode, while BARS already implements the recommended remedy.

**Operational conclusion:** The Hodges literature provides the *justification* for PIPE's BARS design (global anchored ratings rather than binary item checklists), not a warning against it. The design already embodies the Hodges recommendation [S3][S4].

**Caveat (single-study concern):** The medical OSCE literature and IO behavioral interview literature have different validity targets. The Hodges finding is consistently replicated within medical education but has not been directly replicated in employment interview contexts to the author's knowledge. This is a gap — see §Open Questions.

## 4. Anchor-Writing Rules (Observable-Behavior Protocol)

### The core rule: describe actions, not traits

The foundational rule — validated by Smith & Kendall (1963) [S2], operationalized by Flanagan's CIT definition (1954) [S1], and affirmed in every subsequent BARS review — is: **anchors must describe observable behaviors, not inferred traits or evaluative judgments.**

The distinction: "raters are asked to perform less of an inferential leap by focusing on what ratees *do* at work — concrete actions — rather than who they *are* overall — generalised properties, or traits" [S10].

### Documented failure modes

The following anchor-writing failure modes are documented in the literature [S7][S8][S10]:

**Failure mode 1: Evaluative adjectives**
- Bad: "Demonstrates excellent communication" — "excellent" is an evaluative judgment that requires the rater to have already decided the answer before applying the scale.
- Good: "Names the specific stakeholder they contacted, states what information they conveyed, and confirms the outcome of that communication"
- Rule: No adjectives that encode quality levels (good, excellent, poor, strong, weak, thorough).

**Failure mode 2: Inferred mental states**
- Bad: "Shows genuine curiosity about the problem" — "genuine" and "curiosity" are inferences about internal states.
- Good: "Asks at least two follow-up questions about the problem's root cause before proposing a solution"
- Rule: No internal states, intentions, or motivations. Only actions and their externally observable consequences.

**Failure mode 3: Frequency words without operationalization**
- Bad: "Always takes initiative" — "always" is unverifiable in a single interview context.
- Good: "Describes at least one example where they identified a problem before being asked to address it and took action without explicit direction"
- Rule: Frequency modifiers (always, often, sometimes, rarely, never) are only valid when the context makes them verifiable (e.g., in a performance-appraisal BARS covering a sustained period). For interview BARS, replace frequency with specificity of the narrated example.

**Failure mode 4: Double-barrelled anchors**
- Bad: "Describes both a specific situation and quantified outcome" — if only one element is present, the rater cannot score.
- Good: Separate anchors for completeness-of-situation and completeness-of-outcome, or restructure as "Describes a specific situation *with* a quantified outcome and attributable personal role"
- Rule: Each anchor should describe exactly one behavioral profile, not a conjunction of two that might independently occur.

**Failure mode 5: Dimensional leakage**
- Bad: An anchor for "conflict-handling" dimension that says "demonstrates collaborative communication" — the word "collaborative" bleeds into the "collaboration" dimension.
- Rule: Each anchor must be retranslation-testable — it should unambiguously belong to its dimension and not be equally plausible for a neighboring one [S2][S7].

**Failure mode 6: Length-as-quality encoding (verbosity encoding)**
- Bad: A Level 5 anchor for self-awareness that says "Provides a richly detailed narrative..." — this encodes narration length as a quality signal, enabling verbosity bias.
- Good: "Names a specific belief or assumption they held that turned out to be wrong, and describes the specific evidence that changed their view" — qualitatively distinct from lower levels, not quantitatively longer.
- See §5 for full treatment.

### The three-question test for any anchor

Before committing an anchor, ask:
1. **Could a rater observe this behavior without the candidate's stated intent?** (If yes, it's observable.)
2. **Could two raters disagree about whether the behavior occurred, without disagreeing about values?** (If no, it's operationally clear.)
3. **Could this anchor appear under a different dimension without loss of coherence?** (If yes, retranslate — it may leak.)

## 5. Verbosity Bias Mitigation at the Anchor Level

### The problem: length as a proxy for quality

Verbosity bias — the systematic tendency to rate longer responses higher — operates at two levels: (a) **human rater bias**, documented in interview research as the "good story" effect, and (b) **LLM judge bias**, documented at high statistical significance in AI evaluation literature.

For LLM judges specifically, Hu et al. (2024) [S11] found that longer responses achieved 50–65% preference rates vs. ~10–35% for concise responses of equivalent quality. The mechanism: LLMs reward "information mass" (content volume) conflated with "desirability" (content quality). GPT-4o was found to prefer the longer completion 85% of the time when two responses differed by ~200 tokens [S12].

In structured interview contexts, verbal fluency and response elaboration are documented correlates of hireability judgments — but these effects are *reduced* (not eliminated) by BARS [S5][S13]. The research finding from the parent brief that verbosity bias must stay r < 0.10 (BC-20) is correct as a target; here is the anchor-level mechanism for achieving it.

### Anchor-level mitigation strategies

**Strategy 1: Anchor on qualitative specificity, not quantity.**
The distinguishing feature between Level 3 and Level 5 should be the *kind* of information provided, not the *amount*. Example (conflict-handling dimension):
- Level 3: "Describes the disagreement and states their own position clearly"
- Level 5: "Names the specific point of disagreement, states their own position and the reasoning behind it, and describes what they changed based on the other person's argument"
The Level 5 anchor is not simply "more of the same" — it adds a structurally distinct element (change-in-own-position) that cannot be faked by adding words [S7][S8].

**Strategy 2: Use structural markers, not length markers.**
Instead of "provides a comprehensive account," use "includes: a named situation, a named personal action, and a named outcome." Structural completeness can be assessed at any response length.

**Strategy 3: Penalise padding explicitly in the scoring prompt.**
For LLM scoring: the rubric prompt should state "Do not reward response length or narrative elaboration — score only the structural elements named in each anchor level." This is consistent with the mitigation strategies in Hu et al. (2024): explicit criteria specification that separates quality from quantity [S11].

**Strategy 4: Calibration exemplars at equal length.**
When constructing the L/M/H exemplars (R4's scope), the three exemplars should be matched for approximate word count so LLM judges are forced to distinguish them on structural content, not length. (Note: R4 owns the full 3-shot exemplar protocol — this is a handoff note only.)

**Strategy 5: Retranslation check for length correlation.**
After generating draft anchors, compute the word count of each anchor text. A significant Pearson r between anchor word count and anchor scale level (e.g., r > 0.40 across the 5 levels) signals that the anchors themselves encode verbosity. Revise until the correlation falls below 0.20 [derived from S7][S11].

## 6. [UNSOURCED] Maurer 2002 Investigation — RESOLVED

### Status: RESOLVED — CLAIM IS ACCURATE AND SOURCEABLE

**The original claim in parent brief §1.3:**
> "Additional finding from Maurer 2002 [UNSOURCED]: When BARS are used, the difference between expert and novice raters disappears — BARS equalize rater quality."

### The actual paper

**Full citation:** Maurer, S. D. (2002). A practitioner-based analysis of interviewer job expertise and scale format as contextual factors in situational interviews. *Personnel Psychology, 55*(2), 307–327. doi:10.1111/j.1744-6570.2002.tb00112.x [S6]

**Author:** Steven D. Maurer, College of Business and Public Administration, Old Dominion University.

### What the paper actually found

Two police-officer samples (job-content experts) and a student sample (naive raters) scored videotaped interview performances for police sergeant/lieutenant positions using either a BARS scale or a traditional (non-anchored) rating format.

Key findings [S6]:
1. **BARS produced significantly higher interrater agreement and rating accuracy** than the traditional format — for both expert and naive samples.
2. **Job expertise did not produce better ratings than naive raters** when a BARS scale was used. Expert raters using BARS achieved essentially the same accuracy as naive raters using BARS.
3. **Job expertise provided a meaningful advantage *only* when using the traditional, non-anchored format** — i.e., when raters lack behavioral anchors to guide them, domain knowledge helps. BARS eliminates this advantage because the anchors do the cognitive work that expertise provides.

### Is the parent brief's formulation accurate?

**Yes, with one precision note.** The brief states "the difference between expert and novice raters disappears" — this is accurate as a summary. The precise finding is that BARS reduces rater error variance to the point where domain expertise provides no additional incremental accuracy. The claim is not overstated.

**Recommended corrected citation for the parent brief:**
> "Maurer, S. D. (2002). A practitioner-based analysis of interviewer job expertise and scale format as contextual factors in situational interviews. *Personnel Psychology, 55*(2), 307–327. Job-expert (police) and naive-rater (student) samples achieved equivalent accuracy when scoring via BARS, but experts outperformed naives under traditional rating formats — demonstrating that well-designed anchors substitute for domain expertise."

### Implication for PIPE

This finding is directly load-bearing for PIPE's use of LLM judges. An LLM judge is the functional equivalent of a "naive rater" with high linguistic competence but no hiring-domain expertise. Well-designed BARS anchors (per the protocols in §§1–4 of this document) are the mechanism by which the LLM judge can match human expert accuracy — as Maurer's research predicts.

## 7. Verbal Anchor Literature for 5-Point Scales

### Reilly et al. 2006 — RESOLVED

**Full citation:** Reilly, N. P., Bocketti, S. P., Maser, S. A., & Wennet, C. L. (2006). Benchmarks affect perceptions of prior disability in a structured interview. *Journal of Business and Psychology, 20*(4), 489–500. [S14]

**What the paper is:** An experimental study showing that subtle disability-related information (prior depression, substance abuse, or cancer) creates rating bias in unstructured hiring, but that **benchmarked (BARS) rating scales enhance attention to relevant job behaviors and remove the disability bias** [S14].

**What the paper establishes about BARS:** Use of behaviorally anchored benchmarks in structured interviews increases attention to job-relevant behavioral criteria and reduces rater susceptibility to irrelevant biographical information. This operationally confirms the "BARS reduces bias" mechanism by demonstrating it in a specific experimental manipulation.

**What the paper does NOT establish:** It does not specifically examine 5-point vs 7-point scale length, nor does it directly validate verbal anchor wording. The parent brief cited it as evidence for a "5-point fully-anchored BARS" — that citation is slightly over-extended. The paper supports BARS as a bias-reduction mechanism, not 5-point scales specifically.

### 5-point vs 7-point scale: what the evidence actually says

The parent brief's "5-point fully-anchored BARS" recommendation is grounded in practice norms and the general psychometric literature, not in a single study:

- **Reliability curve:** Psychometric meta-analysis finds reliability increases from 2-point through ~7-point scales, with diminishing returns above 7. Five- and seven-point scales produce the most reliable results [S15].
- **Bipolar vs. unipolar:** Bipolar scales (disagree–agree) perform best with 7 points; unipolar scales (never–always, or BARS performance scales) perform best with 5 [S15].
- **Interview-specific:** The structured interview literature (Campion et al. 1997 [S16]; Taylor & Small 2002 [S5]) uses 5-point BARS as a practice norm. No meta-analytic comparison of 5- vs 7-point interview BARS specifically exists in the literature to this researcher's knowledge.
- **LLM judge consideration:** For LLM scoring, 5-point scales provide a larger discrimination range than 3-point while avoiding the precision that LLMs handle unreliably (models tend to cluster at scale midpoints on 7-point scales, effectively creating a 3-point distribution in practice — this is inference from general LLM calibration literature, not a published interview study).

**Recommendation:** The 5-point BARS recommendation is well-supported by practice norms and the reliability curve, but should not be cited to Reilly et al. 2006 specifically. Cite Campion et al. 1997 [S16] and the general psychometric literature [S15] instead.

## Operational Protocol (Handoff for Rubric Authoring)

The following is a step-by-step protocol for R2, R3, and the Lead to use when writing actual anchors from published competency frameworks and culture-dimension instruments.

### Phase 1: Dimension locking (already done)
The 10 dimensions are locked (5 behavioral competencies from parent brief §6.3; 5 culture dimensions from `knowledge/culture/dimensions/`). Do not reopen this.

### Phase 2: Critical incident corpus generation
For each dimension:
1. Pull 10–20 representative interview response excerpts from development runs or calibration transcripts (or use SME-authored vignettes if no transcripts exist yet).
2. Identify 3–6 incidents at each performance level: clearly Level 1 examples, clearly Level 5 examples, and middling examples for Levels 2–4.
3. Record each incident in the format: **[Situation] / [Action taken] / [Outcome]**. Strip the situation and outcome to bare minimum — the *action* is the anchor candidate.

### Phase 3: Anchor drafting from published frameworks (R2/R3 scope)
For each of the 10 dimensions, R2 and R3 will supply anchor language from validated frameworks (Lominger/Korn Ferry, SHL UCF, OCAI, etc.). Before accepting this language, apply the **three-question test** from §4:
1. Is it observable without intent inference?
2. Can two independent raters agree it occurred?
3. Does it belong unambiguously to this dimension?

Reject any anchor that fails any test. Note failure mode (evaluative adjective / inferred mental state / frequency unoperationalized / double-barrelled / dimension leakage).

### Phase 4: Anchor deduplication and level assignment
For each dimension, aim for 5 anchors (one per scale level). Each anchor must be:
- **Qualitatively distinct** from adjacent levels (not just a quantitative amplification)
- **Verifiable** from a single interview response (not requiring multi-encounter observation)
- **Length-neutral** — check word count of each anchor; correlation across 5 levels should be < 0.20

### Phase 5: Retranslation validation (minimum viable)
Have 3–5 reviewers (not the authors) independently:
1. Assign each anchor to the dimension they believe it describes (dimensionality check — target ≥ 70% agreement)
2. Rate each anchor's performance level (1–5 or 1–9) without seeing the assigned level (level check — target SD < 1.5)
Discard anchors failing either test. Replace from the surplus incident corpus.

### Phase 6: Verbosity audit
Compute word count for each anchor. If Pearson r(anchor_level, word_count) > 0.40 across the 5 anchors for any dimension, revise the high-level anchors to remove padding language and revise the low-level anchors to add structural specificity markers (what was absent, what question was not answered).

### Phase 7: 3-shot exemplar linkage (R4 scope)
For each dimension, tag the L/M/H exemplar transcripts to the corresponding anchor levels. Verify the exemplars are matched for approximate word count (±30%). Hand off to R4 for the calibration protocol.

## Open Questions / Gaps

1. **Hodges finding in non-clinical behavioral interviews (unresolved gap):** No published study directly replicates the checklist-vs-global paradox in employment interview (non-clinical) settings. The translation from OSCE to behavioral interview is theoretically warranted but empirically unverified. Future validation work could test whether PIPE's binary probe scoring (if any is added) produces the same expert-penalisation effect.

2. **LLM verbosity bias in structured rubric scoring:** The r < 0.10 target (BC-20) for verbosity correlation is borrowed from the parent brief — no published study specifically establishes this threshold for LLM-scored behavioral interviews. The target is reasonable as a design goal but is not empirically calibrated to PIPE's specific scoring context. R4 should address this via κ calibration experiments.

3. **Reilly et al. 2006 and 5-point scale:** The parent brief's citation of Reilly 2006 for the 5-point recommendation is slightly over-extended. The paper validates BARS as a bias-reduction mechanism but does not specifically compare 5-point to 7-point structured interview scales. This is a minor citation hygiene issue, not a substantive finding gap.

4. **Online crowdsourcing reliability for anchor generation (Kell 2017):** Kell et al. found crowdsourced incidents were usable but note this is "exploratory." The approach has not been replicated at scale for non-social-skill dimensions or for culture dimensions specifically. PIPE should treat this as a provisional method and apply retranslation validation.

# Research: AI Literacy as a Defined Concept and Workforce Skill Gap

## Key Findings

### 1. How is "AI literacy" defined across research and frameworks? What competencies does it include?

AI literacy has converged on a core definition across multiple frameworks, though nuances exist between general AI literacy and generative AI literacy specifically.

**Core Definition (OECD/EC 2025):** AI literacy represents "the technical knowledge, durable skills, and future-ready attitudes required to thrive in a world influenced by AI. It enables learners to engage, create with, manage, and design AI, while critically evaluating its benefits, risks, and ethical implications" [S1]. This definition builds on earlier EU AI Act, OECD, UNESCO, and other organizational frameworks [S1].

**Academic Foundation (Long & Magerko 2020):** The seminal framework defines AI literacy as "a set of competencies that enables individuals to critically evaluate AI technologies; communicate and collaborate effectively with AI; and use AI as a tool online, at home, and in the workplace" [S2]. This framework proposed five overarching themes with 17 specific competencies organized around fundamental questions: "What is AI?", "What can AI do?", "How does AI work?", "How should AI be used?", and "How do people perceive AI?" [S2].

**Distinction from Competency (Chiu et al. 2024):** Recent research emphasizes that AI literacy (knowing what skills) differs from AI competency (applying knowledge beneficially with confidence) [S3]. Literacy involves foundational knowledge and understanding, while competency encompasses five abilities incorporating confidence and performance evaluation [S3].

**Competency Components (Chiu et al. 2024):** A comprehensive framework for K-12 AI education identifies five key components [S3]:
1. Technology — technical foundations and systems understanding
2. Impact — understanding AI's effects on society and individuals
3. Ethics — moral reasoning and responsible AI use
4. Collaboration — working effectively with others on AI projects
5. Self-Reflection — metacognitive awareness and adaptive mindsets

**Four-Domain Structure (OECD/EC 2025):** The AILit Framework organizes AI literacy into four practical domains encompassing 22 competences [S1]:
1. **Engaging with AI** — using AI as a tool to access content, information, or recommendations; requires learners to first recognize AI's presence, then evaluate accuracy and relevance of AI outputs
2. **Creating with AI** — collaborating with AI in creative or problem-solving processes; involves guiding and refining AI output through prompts and feedback
3. **Managing AI** — intentionally choosing how AI can support and enhance human work; includes assigning structured tasks to AI and simulating roles
4. **Designing AI** — understanding how AI works and connecting to social and ethical impacts by shaping how AI systems function

**Cognitive Dimensions (Almatrafi et al. 2024):** AI literacy competencies are identified as recognizing, knowing and understanding, using and applying, evaluating, creating, and navigating ethically [S4].

**ABCE Framework (Ng et al. 2024):** The AI Literacy Questionnaire (AILQ) measures literacy across four dimensions [S5]:
- Affective learning (intrinsic motivation and self-efficacy)
- Behavioral learning (behavioral commitment and collaboration)
- Cognitive learning (know and understand; apply, evaluate and create)
- Ethical learning

**Generative AI Literacy — Twelve Competencies (Annapureddy et al. 2024):** Generative AI literacy is defined as "a long-term, continuous process by which several core competencies, allowing to effectively and responsibly understand, assess, and work with generative AI technologies, have been identified, acquired, practiced, and mastered" [S6]. The twelve competencies are [S6]:
1. Basic AI Literacy
2. Knowledge of Generative AI Models
3. Knowledge of Capacity and Limitations
4. Skill to Use Generative AI Tools
5. Ability to Detect AI-Generated Content
6. Ability to Assess Output
7. Skill in Prompting (Prompt Engineering)
8. Ability to Program and Fine-Tune
9. Knowledge of Contexts of Use
10. Knowledge of Ethical Implications
11. Knowledge of Legal Aspects
12. Ability to Continuously Learn

**Generative AI Literacy — Five Dimensions (Liu et al. 2025):** Research identifies five core dimensions of Generative AI Literacy (GAIL) [S7]:
1. Basic Technical Proficiency — understanding fundamental principles and operational techniques
2. Prompt Optimization — designing and refining prompts to improve AI output quality
3. Content Evaluation — assessing accuracy, reliability, and coherence of AI-generated content
4. Innovative Application — transforming creative ideas into practical outcomes using AI
5. Ethical and Compliance Awareness — identifying risks and adhering to regulations and professional norms

**Interdisciplinary Nature:** AI literacy requires "weaving together threads of understanding from computation, mathematics, statistics, data handling, language, visual perception, ethics, critical thinking, human cognition, and specific application domains" [S8].

### 2. What does research say about the AI literacy skill gap in the workforce?

Research reveals a significant and growing gap between AI adoption and workforce preparedness across multiple dimensions.

**Scale of Reskilling Need (IBM 2024):** CEOs estimate that 35% of their workforce will require retraining and reskilling over the next three years — up dramatically from just 6% in 2021 [S9]. IBM reports that as much as 40% of the global workforce will need to reskill in the next three years as AI adoption accelerates, potentially affecting 1.4 billion of the 3.4 billion people in the global workforce [S10].

**Adoption-Training Mismatch (Multiple Sources 2024):** While 75% of companies are adopting AI, only 35% of talent have received AI training in the last year [S11]. A LinkedIn survey found that while 61% of desk workers want AI training, less than half of companies have begun formal education efforts [S11, S12]. This reveals an urgent skills gap where worker demand for training far exceeds organizational supply.

**Organizational Readiness Gaps (2024 Research):**
- 59% of enterprise leaders say their organization has an AI skills gap in 2026 [S13]
- 34% of surveyed business leaders reported their organizations are "significantly under-resourced" or "under-resourced" when it comes to in-house AI talent [S11]
- Nearly half (49%) of leaders identify a lack of skilled talent as a primary barrier to successful AI implementation [S11]
- Only 17% of business leaders believe their employees have "advanced" skills that fully leverage AI tools [S11]
- Only 35% of organizations report having a mature, workforce-wide upskilling program [S11]

**Worker Barriers (2024):** Workers are eager to learn, but 74% say a lack of training is holding them back [S13].

**Awareness Disparities (LinkedIn 2024):** While 89% of business leaders are at least familiar with generative AI, only 68% of workers have reached this level [S13].

**Training Supply Insufficiency (OECD 2025):** The report "Bridging the AI skills gap: Is training keeping up?" found that between 0.3% and 5.5% of analyzed training courses in countries like Australia, Germany, Singapore, and the United States deliver AI content [S14]. This suggests current training supply may not be sufficient to meet demand, especially the growing demand for general AI literacy [S14].

**AI Exposure vs. Skills Requirement (OECD 2025):** One in three job vacancies have high AI exposure, yet only about 1% of these jobs require specific, complex AI skills [S14]. The vast majority of workers exposed to AI will not require specialized AI skills — most workers across the OECD only require a general understanding of AI [S14].

**Policy Response Gap (OECD 2025):** Among 21 analyzed countries, 14 have invested in AI-specific training programmes, with 9 focusing on developing AI professionals and 7 aiming to build AI literacy amongst the general public [S14]. Countries rely heavily on financial incentives to promote AI skill development, underutilizing other policy tools that could support a more inclusive transition [S14].

**Skills Obsolescence Concerns (IBM 2024):** 57% of respondents believe existing skills will become obsolete by 2030, 67% say job roles are becoming shorter-lived, and 42% anticipate AI-based productivity gains [S10].

**Employment Impact Despite Gap (PwC 2025):** Occupations with lower exposure to AI saw strong job growth (65%) in recent years (2019-2024), and growth remained robust even in more exposed occupations (38%) [S15]. Job numbers are growing in every industry analyzed across both classifications [S15].

**Wage Premiums for AI Skills (PwC 2025):** Jobs which require AI skills offer a wage premium over similar roles that don't require AI skills in every industry analyzed, with the average premium hitting 56%, up from 25% last year [S15].

**Degree Requirements Declining (PwC 2025):** The percentage of AI-augmented jobs requiring a degree fell from 66% in 2019 to 59% in 2024 [S15]. Organizations are increasingly prioritizing demonstrated skills and learning aptitude over credentials [S15].

**ROI of Structured Programs:** Organizations pairing AI investment with structured workforce capability building are nearly twice as likely to see strong returns [S11]. AI-optimized personalized learning pathways can reduce time-to-proficiency by up to 40% compared to commodified training [S13].

**Organizational Examples (2024):** Organizations like IKEA and Mastercard are already implementing company-wide AI literacy programs, offering AI fundamentals training and ethical AI modules to ensure employees understand responsible AI use [S13].

**Leadership Replacement vs. Retraining Preference (Deloitte):** Leaders are 3.1× more likely to prefer replacing employees with new AI-ready talent versus retraining existing workforce [S15].

**Student Skill Gap (Vodafone Foundation 2024):** A 2024 study of 12- to 17-year-olds across Europe reported that 74% believe AI will play a significant role in their professional lives, but only 46% think their schools adequately prepare them for AI [S16]. Additionally, 49% of 17- to 27-year-olds struggle with critically evaluating and identifying AI's shortfalls, such as whether AI systems can invent facts [S1].

### 3. Is "reasoning about AI output" (evaluating, critiquing, debugging AI-generated content) captured in any framework?

Yes, critical evaluation of AI output is explicitly captured across multiple frameworks as a core competency, though terminology varies.

**OECD/EC AILit Framework (2025):** The "Engaging with AI" domain explicitly requires learners to "first recognize AI's presence, then evaluate the accuracy and relevance of AI outputs" [S1]. Learners must "develop a foundational understanding of AI's technical foundations in order to critically analyze its capabilities and limitations" [S1].

**Critical Evaluation in "Creating with AI" Domain (OECD/EC 2025):** This domain "involves guiding and refining AI output through prompts and feedback, while ensuring the content remains fair and appropriate" and "involves ethical considerations related to content ownership, attribution, and the responsible use of existing materials" [S1].

**Long & Magerko (2020):** Among the 17 AI competencies, "Critically Interpreting Data" and understanding "AI's Strengths & Weaknesses" explicitly address evaluating AI outputs [S2].

**Generative AI Literacy — Content Evaluation Dimension (Liu et al. 2025):** The third dimension of GAIL specifically focuses on "Content Evaluation" — assessing accuracy, reliability, and coherence of AI-generated content [S7]. This dimension emphasizes users' critical thinking in assessing AI-generated outputs, based on critical thinking theory and information literacy frameworks [S17].

**Generative AI Literacy — Assessment Competency (Annapureddy et al. 2024):** Competency 6, "Ability to Assess Output," focuses on critically evaluating generated results for quality and accuracy [S6]. The authors emphasize assessing "whether the generated content is relevant and useful" and verifying facts to "prevent or minimize the effect of model hallucinations" [S6].

**Digital Promise AI Literacy Framework:** The framework's "Types of Use" include "Critical Appraisal" as a distinct mode of engagement [S18].

**SNAIL Scale (Laupichler et al. 2023):** The "Scale for the assessment of non-experts' AI literacy" includes "Critical Appraisal" as one of three underlying dimensions alongside Technical Understanding and Practical Application [S19]. The final questionnaire consists of 31 items measuring these dimensions [S19].

**MAILS (Carolus et al. 2023; Koch et al. 2024):** The Meta AI Literacy Scale measures AI ethics (3 items) as part of the AI Literacy Core, which encompasses critical reasoning about AI systems [S20].

**Rubric-Based Assessment (University of Calgary):** A practical AI Literacy and Critical Thinking Self-Assessment Rubric provides a three-level framework [S21]:
- Beginning: students accept AI outputs without questioning
- Developing: inconsistent identification of errors with support
- Proficient: independent evaluation with intentional adjustments

**AI Competency Objective Scale (AICOS):** This advanced instrument with 51 multiple-choice items integrates critical thinking as a core capability, defining AI competency not only as technical proficiency but as a broader set of capabilities that encompass critical thinking, ethical reflection, digital agency, and self-regulated learning [S22].

**Supporting Literacies for Critical Evaluation:** Research identifies that multiple literacies support critical evaluation [S23]:
- Ethical literacy provides philosophical frameworks and critical reasoning skills necessary to identify dilemmas, analyze potential harms and benefits, and advocate for responsible AI development and deployment
- Media literacy empowers individuals to analyze claims about AI capabilities critically, identify biases in outputs or reporting, discern deepfakes or AI-generated text, and evaluate the broader societal and political implications of AI adoption

**Educational Focus on Critical Analysis (2024 Research):** Critical analysis tasks require students to "analyze AI-generated content, evaluate its accuracy, and identify potential biases or limitations, moving them from passive consumers to active evaluators of AI outputs" [S17].

**Concern About Over-Reliance:** Research emphasizes that "overreliant users would benefit from training in verification, self-regulation, and critical evaluation," and "it is crucial to ensure that AI is used as a cognitive amplifier rather than a replacement for critical analysis" [S22].

**Assessment Methods — Performance vs. Self-Report:** Conventional AI literacy assessments often rely on self-reported surveys, which are effective in capturing perceived knowledge but may lack the reliability needed to accurately measure actual competencies, especially given the tendency for individuals to overestimate their understanding [S17]. In contrast, performance-based assessments evaluate actual competencies through direct engagement, offering a more reliable measure of skills [S17].

### 4. Are there any published assessments or rubrics for measuring AI literacy?

Yes, multiple validated assessment instruments and rubrics have been published between 2023-2025, employing both self-reported and performance-based methodologies.

**GLAT — GenAI Literacy Assessment Test (Jin et al. 2025):** A 20-item multiple-choice instrument designed to measure generative AI literacy among higher education students, developed following established procedures in psychological and educational measurement [S24]. The researchers utilized Classical Test Theory analysis and Item Response Theory (IRT) modeling with a 2-parameter logistic (2PL) model with 355 higher education student responses [S24]. Psychometric results: Cronbach's alpha = 0.80, Omega total = 0.81, RMSEA = 0.03, CFI = 0.97 [S24]. The instrument demonstrated robust factor structure and significantly predicted performance on GenAI-supported tasks, outperforming self-reported measures like perceived ChatGPT proficiency [S24].

**GAIL Scale (Liu et al. 2025):** A comprehensive generative AI literacy scale measuring five dimensions (Basic Technical Proficiency, Prompt Optimization, Content Evaluation, Innovative Application, Ethical and Compliance Awareness) [S7]. Multi-stage development approach [S7]:
- AI-Assisted Item Generation using NLP models and Claude 3.5 Sonnet API with BERT-based semantic similarity assessment
- Expert Validation by three domain experts
- Exploratory Factor Analysis (EFA): 278 private sector employees; KMO = 0.915; cumulative variance = 89.746%
- Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA): 306 private sector employees; model fit: χ² = 136.937, CFI = 0.995, RMSEA = 0.029
- Cronbach's α = 0.943 (excellent internal consistency)
- All dimensions exceeded CR > 0.7 and AVE > 0.5

The GAIL scale demonstrated significant positive impact on job performance (β = 0.680, p < 0.001), with Creative Self-Efficacy partially mediating this relationship (indirect effect = 0.537) [S7].

**Meta AI Literacy Scale (MAILS) (Carolus et al. 2023; Koch et al. 2024):** A self-assessment instrument for AI literacy and additional psychological competencies conducive for the use of AI [S20]. Scale specifications:
- Original version: 34 items
- Short version (newly developed): 10 items
- Response Format: 11-point Likert scale (0-10)

Primary dimensions measured [S20]:
1. AI Literacy Core (18 items): Using and applying AI (6), Knowledge and understanding of AI (6), Detecting AI (3), AI ethics (3)
2. Create AI Ability (4 items, measured as correlate)
3. Supporting Psychological Competencies (12 items): AI self-efficacy, AI self-competency

Validation results confirmed most proposed axioms through three empirical studies, with the short version achieving good model fit indices (RMSEA = .090, SRMR = .038, CFI = .958) [S20]. The total AI literacy score correlated with an objective AI literacy test, and positive relationships were found between MAILS scores and prior AI use and learning opportunities [S20].

**AILQ — AI Literacy Questionnaire (Ng et al. 2024):** A 32-item self-reported questionnaire measuring students' literacy development across four dimensions (affective, behavioral, cognitive, ethical learning), validated through rigorous psychometric analysis [S5].

**SNAIL — Scale for the assessment of non-experts' AI literacy (Laupichler et al. 2023):** Developed to measure AI competencies among individuals without formal AI or computer science backgrounds [S19]. Three-factor model identified through exploratory factor analysis:
1. Technical Understanding
2. Critical Appraisal
3. Practical Application

Final instrument contains 31 items (reduced from original set after eliminating items with high intercorrelations and low communalities) [S19]. SNAIL is designed to assess individual and group AI literacy levels while enabling evaluation of educational program effectiveness [S19].

**AICOS — AI Competency Objective Scale:** An advanced instrument for measuring AI literacy with 51 multiple-choice items ensuring efficient implementation and standardized evaluation [S22]. Integrates current AI literacy and measurement models.

**Quality Assessment of Scales (Systematic Review):** A systematic review of AI literacy scales evaluated measurement properties using the COSMIN tool, assessing content validity, structural validity, internal consistency, reliability, construct validity, and responsiveness [S25]. Overall, scales demonstrated good structural validity and internal consistency, though only a few have been tested for content validity, reliability, construct validity, and responsiveness [S17, S25].

**Rubric-Based Approaches:** Simple 3-level rubrics can be co-created with students to build ownership and clarify expectations [S21]. Example tracking rubric includes:
- Beginning: students accept AI outputs without questioning
- Developing: inconsistent identification of errors with support
- Proficient: independent evaluation with intentional adjustments

**PISA 2029 Assessment (OECD):** The OECD is developing the PISA 2029 Media and Artificial Intelligence Literacy assessment, which will provide international benchmarking data for AI literacy among 15-year-olds globally [S26].

**Comprehensive Assessment Dimensions:** A comprehensive AI literacy assessment should evaluate whether individuals [S17]:
- Understand where and how AI appears across tools
- Can judge accuracy, quality, alignment, and potential bias in AI-generated content
- Understand safe data handling, age-appropriate use, and risk mitigation

**Organizational Assessment Tools (Benlian & Pinski 2025):** The AI Literacy Development Canvas provides strategic planning tools for organizations [S27]:
- AI Literacy Assessment Matrix: diagnostic instrument tailored to different management tiers (executive, middle management, non-IT staff)
- AI Literacy Development Canvas: strategic planning tool guiding organizations on when, where, and how to invest in AI training and capability building

The framework emphasizes three essential capability areas: conceptual knowledge of AI technologies, ethical considerations in AI deployment, and practical application skills [S27].

## Direct Implications for the Project

- **Assessment Design:** Multiple validated instruments (GLAT, GAIL, MAILS, SNAIL) provide methodological templates for building AI literacy assessments that go beyond self-report to measure actual competency through performance tasks. The GLAT's finding that objective measures outperform self-reported proficiency [S24] is directly relevant to designing skill evaluation challenges.

- **Critical Evaluation as Core Skill:** Every major framework includes critical evaluation of AI output as a central competency [S1, S6, S7, S19], not an optional add-on. This validates prioritizing challenges that assess candidates' ability to detect AI hallucinations, assess output quality, and refine prompts — skills that research shows 49% of young adults struggle with [S1, S16].

- **Workforce Urgency:** The dramatic increase in reskilling estimates (6% to 35% in three years [S9]) and the adoption-training gap (75% adoption vs. 35% trained [S11]) suggest strong market demand for platforms that can efficiently assess and verify AI literacy at scale, particularly given that 74% of workers report training barriers [S13].

- **Competency vs. Literacy Distinction:** The clear research distinction between knowing about AI (literacy) and applying it effectively (competency) [S3] suggests assessments should include both knowledge-check components and applied performance tasks that demonstrate real-world usage under realistic constraints.

- **ROI of Structured Assessment:** Organizations pairing AI investment with structured capability building are nearly 2× more likely to see strong returns [S11], and structured programs can reduce time-to-proficiency by 40% [S13]. This provides a clear value proposition for rigorous, research-backed assessment tools that identify specific capability gaps rather than generic "AI skills" labels.

## Open Questions / Gaps

- **Longitudinal Skill Retention:** While multiple instruments measure AI literacy at a point in time, research on how these competencies degrade or strengthen over time with varying levels of practice is sparse. The "Ability to Continuously Learn" [S6] is identified as critical but not yet well-measured.

- **Cross-Cultural Validity:** Most validated instruments were developed in Western contexts (US, Europe, Australia). The OECD framework aims for global applicability [S1], but cross-cultural validation data for most assessment instruments is limited.

- **Competency Thresholds for Job Roles:** While frameworks identify what AI literacy entails, research on minimum competency thresholds for specific job families (e.g., "What level of prompt optimization is required for a marketing manager vs. a data analyst?") remains underdeveloped. The OECD finding that only 1% of AI-exposed jobs require complex AI skills [S14] suggests most roles need general literacy, but specific cut-scores are not research-defined.

- **Impact of Generative AI on Earlier Frameworks:** The Long & Magerko (2020) framework [S2] predates ChatGPT and generative AI ubiquity. While newer frameworks address generative AI specifically [S6, S7], the field lacks consensus on whether these represent entirely new competency domains or extensions of existing ones.

- **Causal Evidence on Job Performance:** While Liu et al. (2025) demonstrate a strong correlation between GAIL and job performance (β = 0.680) [S7], causal evidence from controlled experiments or natural experiments is limited. Most workforce studies rely on cross-sectional surveys rather than longitudinal or experimental designs.

- **Rubric Reliability Across Evaluators:** Practical rubrics exist [S21], but inter-rater reliability data for human evaluators scoring AI literacy performance tasks is not widely published. This is critical for scaling assessment systems that require human judgment.

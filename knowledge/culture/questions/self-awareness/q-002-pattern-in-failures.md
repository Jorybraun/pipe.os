## Question

When you look back over your career, what's a pattern you've noticed in the situations where you've struggled or failed? Not a single incident — a recurring thing.

## Why we ask this

A single failure story is easy. Recognizing a *pattern* across multiple failures requires standing back from specifics and seeing yourself as an object of analysis. This is one of the clearest discriminators of mature self-awareness. The question also tests whether the candidate treats their own failures as data rather than as things to be minimized.

Weaker candidates produce a pattern that is secretly a strength ("I struggle when I'm not challenged enough") or refuse to identify a pattern ("every situation is different").

## BARS rubric

- **5** — Names a specific, non-flattering pattern. Can give at least two examples from different points in their career where the pattern showed up. Can articulate *why* the pattern exists — what need or default in them produces it. Describes what they do now to notice the pattern earlier, with honesty about whether they've actually succeeded at breaking it.
- **4** — Specific pattern with examples. Some explanation of why. Decent corrective practice.
- **3** — Names a mild pattern with one example. Explanation is thin.
- **2** — Pattern is secretly a strength. Or: pattern is so general it doesn't commit to anything ("sometimes I take on too much").
- **1** — Cannot identify a pattern. Or: refuses the premise ("every situation is different"). Or: describes patterns in others' behavior that caused their failures.

## Calibration examples

### Low (1-2)

> "I think the thing I'd identify is that I sometimes take on more than I can handle because I don't want to say no. It's probably a strength-weakness thing — I care about helping the team, but it means I get stretched thin."

Generic, flattering, "strength-weakness thing" is a tell. Scores 2.

### Medium (3)

> "I've noticed I have a pattern of underestimating how long project handoffs take. I tend to think a project is done when the main code is written, and then the documentation, knowledge transfer, and onboarding of the next owner takes longer than I expected. I've been building more handoff time into my estimates."

Specific, non-flattering, one example pattern, clear corrective. Decent. Scores 3.

### High (4-5)

> "The pattern I've noticed is that I struggle in situations where I feel I should already know the answer. My first month at any new job, I over-claim expertise and under-ask for help — because I took a senior role and I'm worried about looking incompetent. It showed up most visibly at my current job: I spent three weeks trying to debug a performance issue on my own before asking anyone, and I eventually asked a colleague who solved it in forty minutes because he knew a piece of infra history I'd never encountered. It also showed up at my previous job when I joined a team using a framework I'd only skimmed — I wasted about two weeks being subtly confused in meetings before I admitted I didn't fully understand how some core abstractions worked. The pattern is: the higher my title, the more expensive my unwillingness to ask basic questions. What's behind it is some version of impostor syndrome plus ego protection. What I do now is pick one person in my first week on any new team and deliberately ask them something basic, as a way of breaking my own freeze. I can't say I've fully fixed it — I noticed the freeze happening in my current role and had to talk myself past it. But it's shorter than it used to be."

Specific, unflattering, multiple examples from different jobs, honest about the underlying cause, specific corrective that doesn't claim full success. Scores 5.

## Probe library

- *generic / strength-weakness*: "That's pretty common. Is there something more specific — something that might be more particular to you?"
- *single example*: "You mentioned one situation — where else has this shown up?"
- *no causal explanation*: "What do you think is underneath the pattern? What's producing it?"
- *fully fixed*: "Is it actually resolved, or do you still catch yourself doing it?"

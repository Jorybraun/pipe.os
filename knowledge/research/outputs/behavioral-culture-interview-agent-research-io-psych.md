## Predictive Validity of Structured Interviews

### Core Meta-Analytic Estimates

The evidence base spans 100+ years of personnel psychology research integrated via meta-analysis. The three most widely cited benchmarks are:

**1. McDaniel et al. 1994 [S2]** — the landmark meta-analysis of 160 studies (N ≈ 22,000):
- Structured interviews: **ρ = .44** (corrected operational validity)
- Unstructured interviews: **ρ = .33**
- Job-related structured interviews: **ρ = .50**
- Situational interviews specifically: **ρ = .50**
- Validity generalized across jobs and settings

**2. Schmidt & Hunter 1998 [S1]** (Psychological Bulletin, 85 years of research):
- Structured interviews: **ρ = .51**
- Unstructured interviews: **ρ = .38**
- GMA + structured interview combined: **ρ = .63** (multivariate)

**3. Schmidt & Oh 2016 [S3]** (100-year update, working paper):
- With the more accurate indirect range restriction correction (Hunter, Schmidt & Le 2006), the gap closes:
  - Structured interviews: **ρ = .58**
  - Unstructured interviews: **ρ = .58**
- Combined GMA + structured interview: **ρ = .76** (highest validity combination aside from GMA + integrity test at .78)
- Incremental validity of structured interview over GMA alone: **+18%**
- Incremental validity of unstructured interview over GMA: **+13%**

> **⚠️ Methodological note:** The convergence of structured and unstructured interview validity in Schmidt & Oh 2016 is an artifact of applying a more accurate range restriction correction, which amplified unstructured interview estimates substantially. The practical inference from the earlier, more conservative estimates (structured > unstructured) remains the practitioner consensus. The incremental validity advantage for structured remains.

**4. Wiesner & Cronshaw 1988 [S7]** ⚠️ (pre-2000, meta-analysis):
- Individual structured interviews: **ρ = .34**
- Board/panel structured: **ρ = .46**
- Individual unstructured: **ρ = .20**

**5. Huffcutt & Arthur 1994 [S8]** ⚠️ (Hunter & Hunter 1984 revisited):
- Level 1 structure (low): ρ = .20
- Level 2 structure: ρ = .35
- Level 3 structure: ρ = .56
- Level 4 structure (highest): ρ = .57

**6. Salgado & Moscoso 2006** (synthesis across multiple meta-analyses):
- Operative validity coefficient for structured behavioral interviews (SBI): **ρ = .64** for predicting job performance

### Summary Table: Interview Type vs. Validity

| Interview Type | Validity (ρ) | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Highly structured behavioral | .50–.64 | McDaniel 1994 [S2]; Salgado 2006 |
| Structured (any) | .44–.58 | McDaniel 1994 [S2]; Schmidt & Oh 2016 [S3] |
| Situational interview | .50 | McDaniel 1994 [S2] |
| Unstructured | .33–.38 | McDaniel 1994 [S2]; Schmidt & Hunter 1998 [S1] |
| GMA + Structured (combined) | .76 | Schmidt & Oh 2016 [S3] |

### Why Structure Matters

Structure increases validity through four mechanisms [S4]:
1. **Standardization** — same questions to all candidates removes idiosyncratic variance
2. **Job analysis anchoring** — questions derived from job-relevant KSAs reduce construct irrelevance
3. **Behavioral anchoring (BARS)** — scoring guides focus raters on job-related behaviors
4. **Statistical aggregation** — multiple scored responses increase signal-to-noise

Twelve meta-analyses have consistently found structured > unstructured (Conway et al. 1995; Huffcutt & Arthur 1994; McDaniel et al. 1994; Wiesner & Cronshaw 1988; and eight others) [S4].

## Competency Frameworks & Scoring Methods

### BARS (Behaviorally Anchored Rating Scales)

**Taylor & Small 2002** [S5] — meta-analysis of 19 past-behavior interview studies:
- **With BARS:** interrater reliability = **.77**, validity = **.35**
- **Without BARS:** interrater reliability = **.73**, validity = **.26**
- Conclusion: BARS yield ~35% improvement in criterion-related validity

BARS work by:
- Anchoring each rating point with specific behavioral examples derived from job analysis
- Reducing rater discretion — interviewers match responses to anchor descriptions rather than forming holistic impressions
- Especially powerful when every point on the scale is defined (fully-anchored 5-point) vs. only extremes anchored [S4]

**Maurer 2002**: When BARS are used, job experts and naïve raters do not differ in rating accuracy — BARS equalize rater quality [S4].

### STAR Method (Situation-Task-Action-Result)

The STAR structure is the practitioner implementation of **past-behavior questions (PBQs)** — also called Patterned Behavior Description Interview (PBDI, Janz 1982 [S11]).

**Theoretical basis:** Behavioral consistency principle — "the best predictor of future behavior is past behavior in similar situations" (Wernimont & Campbell 1968; Schmidt et al. 2016 [S3]).

**Empirical support from Taylor & Small 2002 [S5]:**
- PBQ uncorrected validity: **r = .31**
- Situational question (SQ) uncorrected validity: **r = .25**
- PBQs outperform SQs, especially for higher-complexity jobs

**Why PBQs work:**
- Require concrete episodic memory retrieval — harder to fabricate convincingly
- Past behavior assesses actual demonstrated competence vs. intended behavior
- STAR structure probes all four components, reducing response incompleteness

### Situational Interviews (SI)

**Theoretical basis:** Goal-setting theory (Locke & Latham 1984) — intentions predict future behavior [S4].

**Key findings:**
- SQ validity: **r = .25–.35** (Taylor & Small 2002; Latham & Sue-Chan 1999) [S5]
- SQs measure primarily **job knowledge** (Conway & Peneno 1999; Day & Carroll 2003)
- SQs show **lower group differences** (d = .20) than PBQs (d = .10) for race [S4]
- For **high-complexity jobs**, SQ validity drops: .18 vs. .27 for low-complexity
- For **higher-level/executive positions**, PBQs maintain validity (.30–.31) while SQs decline

**Best practice [S4]:** Use **both SQ and PBQ** in the same interview — they measure complementary constructs (job knowledge + experience/personality) and together yield higher combined validity than either alone.

### Competency-Based Interviews (CBI)

CBI is the operationalization of **structured behavioral interviewing** around a predefined competency framework. The empirical requirements for valid CBIs:

1. **Job analysis** — identify job-critical competencies via critical incident technique or task analysis [S4]
2. **Question development** — map STAR/PBQ questions to specific competency behavioral indicators
3. **BARS construction** — develop 3–5 level behavioral anchors per question from SME input
4. **Standardization** — same question set, order, and probes for all candidates

**Campion et al. 1997 [S4]** identified 15 validated components of structure across two dimensions:
- **Content dimension** (7 components): job analysis basis, same questions, limit prompting, better question types, longer interview, control ancillary info, no applicant questions until after
- **Evaluation dimension** (8 components): rate each answer, anchored scales, notes, multiple interviewers, same interviewers across candidates, no inter-candidate discussion, training, statistical prediction

Research shows typical interviews use only 6 of these 15 components [S4]. More components = higher validity.

## Biases in Behavioral Interviews

### Race/Ethnicity Differences

**Huffcutt & Roth 1998** [S6] (meta-analysis, 31 studies):
- **White–Black** mean difference: **d = .25** (favoring White applicants)
- **White–Hispanic** mean difference: **d = .26**
- High-structure interviews: **d = .23**
- Low-structure interviews: **d = .32**
- Past-behavior interviews: **d = .10** ← smallest bias
- Situational interviews: **d = .20**

**Critical finding:** Structure significantly reduces racial bias. PBQs show the smallest racial differences of any question type.

**Levashina et al. 2014 [S4]** (meta-analysis of post-1996 studies, N = 121,044):
- Post-1996 field studies showed race group differences: **d = −.01** (essentially zero)
- Combined with pre-1996 studies: **d = .13** overall
- The dramatic reduction post-1996 likely reflects increased adoption of fully structured designs with per-question rating

**Sacco et al. 2003** (large sample, N > 30,000): Small or no race/gender similarity effects in rigorous structured interviews.

### Gender Differences

**Alonso, Moscoso & Salgado 2017 [S9]** (meta-analysis, K=19, N=34,130):
- Structured Behavioral Interview gender difference: **d = −0.16** (small, slightly favoring women)
- 90% credibility value includes zero → no meaningful adverse impact
- Confirmed: SBI does **not** produce adverse impact against women

**Huffcutt et al. 2001 [S4]** (prior meta-analysis):
- Overall interview gender differences: **d = 0.06** (negligible)
- Structured: d ≈ 0; Unstructured/low-structure: **d = 0.23** against women

**Conclusion:** Structured behavioral interviews effectively eliminate gender bias documented in unstructured formats. The more structured the interview, the smaller the group differences.

### Cognitive Biases Documented in Interview Research

| Bias | Mechanism | Mitigation via Structure |
|---|---|---|
| **Halo effect** | One positive trait inflates all ratings | Per-question rating with BARS; delay holistic rating |
| **Recency bias** | Last-heard information weighted over early responses | Structured note-taking throughout; rate each question |
| **Similarity bias** | Interviewers favor candidates demographically similar to themselves | Panel interviews; same-interviewer constraint; BARS anchors |
| **First impression / primacy** | Rapport-building phase biases subsequent evaluations | Limit/eliminate rapport-building; start with structured questions |
| **Impression management** | Self-promotion inflates ratings (r = .26 with interview scores) | Structure reduces IM effectiveness (low-structure: .27 → high-structure: .17) |
| **Contrast effect** | Rating candidates relative to previous interviewees, not absolute standards | Per-candidate independent scoring; BARS anchors |

Sources: Levashina et al. 2014 [S4]; Barrick, Shaffer & DeGrassi 2009; Reilly et al. 2006

**Interviewer training** is a proven bias mitigation: structured interview training improves interrater reliability and reduces systematic rater error [S10]. Note-taking during interviews also significantly reduces recency bias (Carrier & Titus 1979 literature review).

## Construct Validity

### What Are Behavioral Interviews Actually Measuring?

**Huffcutt, Conway, Roth & Stone 2001 [S6]** (meta-analysis of 47 actual interviews, JAP):
- **Mental ability / cognitive load** — most frequently implicitly assessed construct across all interview types
- **Applied social skills** — especially in less-structured interviews
- **Conscientiousness** — the most frequently explicitly assessed personality facet in structured interviews
- **Job knowledge** — especially in situational interviews
- **Experience** — especially in past-behavior interviews
- **Education/training credentials** — in conventional/unstructured

Structured interview ratings for personality facets: conscientiousness (r = .26), extraversion (r = .08), other Big Five traits (r = .01–.12) [S4].

**Schmidt & Oh 2016 [S3]:**
> "Employment interviews probably measure a combination of previous experience, mental ability, and a number of personality traits, such as conscientiousness; in addition, they may measure specific job-related skills and behavior patterns."

Average correlation between unstructured interview scores and GMA: **r = .31** [S3].

**Salgado & Moscoso 2002 [S4]** (comprehensive meta-analysis of construct validity):
- Structured behavioral interviews have a more differentiated construct profile than unstructured
- Incremental validity over GMA and personality tests: **structured interviews provide additive prediction** (Berry, Sackett & Landers 2007; Cortina et al. 2000)

### Incremental Validity Over GMA and Conscientiousness

**Cortina et al. 2000** (Personnel Psychology):
- Interview scores predict job performance **over and above** cognitive ability and conscientiousness
- Incremental validity: interviews add criterion-related variance not captured by GMA or Big Five

**Schmidt & Oh 2016 [S3]:**
- GMA alone: ρ = .65
- Structured interview alone: ρ = .58
- GMA + Structured interview: ρ = .76
- This increment (.11) represents ~17% gain in predictive power

**Key implication for construct interpretation:** Structured behavioral interviews are a *composite* measure — they capture cognitive ability (via quality of reasoning in responses), work-related experience (via concreteness and relevance of STAR examples), conscientiousness (via preparation and thoroughness), and applied social skills (via communication quality). This composite nature explains both their high validity and their incremental value over any single construct measure.

## Interview Design: Length, Structure, Question Count

### Optimal Number of Questions

**Campion et al. 1997 [S4]** component #5: "Use longer interviews or larger number of questions" — explicitly listed as a validity-enhancing structure component.

**Research consensus:** More questions → higher reliability via internal consistency and interrater agreement. The psychometric principle of test length (Spearman-Brown prophecy formula) applies: reliability increases as more items are added.

**Conway, Jako & Goodman 1995 [S4]** (meta-analysis of interrater reliability):
- Structured interviews: average interrater reliability = **r = .73**
- Unstructured interviews: **r = .37–.40**
- Panel structured interviews: **r = .76** (additional reliability from multiple raters)

**Huffcutt, Culbertson & Weyhrauch 2013** (meta-analytic reliability estimates by structure):
- SBI interrater reliability ≈ **.73–.79** (consistent across multiple meta-analyses)
- Unstructured: **.37–.40**

**Thorsteinson 2018** (meta-analysis of interview length effects, J. Occupational & Organizational Psychology):
- Longer interviews → higher reliability and validity
- Effect holds across interview types
- The relationship is not infinite — diminishing returns appear; optimal range appears to be 30–60 minutes

**Practical design guidelines (derived from empirical evidence):**

| Parameter | Recommendation | Evidence Base |
|---|---|---|
| Questions per competency dimension | 2–4 STAR/behavioral questions | Reliability via aggregation [S4] |
| Total questions | 6–12 structured questions | Campion et al. 1997 [S4]; practical constraint |
| Interview duration | 30–60 minutes | Thorsteinson 2018; Campion et al. 1997 |
| Rating scale points | 5-point fully-anchored BARS | Reilly et al. 2006; Taylor & Small 2002 [S5] |
| Number of interviewers | 2–3 (panel) | Conway et al. 1995; reliability gain |
| Interview structure components | ≥7 of 15 Campion components | Levashina et al. 2014 [S4] |

**Note on question count vs. interview length tradeoff:** Research shows that 5-8 competency-targeted questions with full STAR probing typically fills 45–60 minutes. Using both SQ and PBQ formats (2–3 each per major competency) is superior to either type alone [S4].

### Structure Component Priority (Empirical Rank-Order)

Most frequently used and reliability-enhancing components [S4]:
1. **Job analysis basis** (71% of field studies use this) — foundational
2. **Same questions to all** (74%) — removes between-interviewer variance
3. **Better question types** (STAR/SI, 78%) — content validity
4. **Anchored rating scales** (66%) — reduces rater subjectivity
5. **Rate each question** (71%) — prevents halo
6. **Interviewer training** (52%) — reduces systematic rater bias

## Seniority-Level Differences in Interview Design

### Job Complexity as Moderator

**Taylor & Small 2002 [S5]** (meta-analysis, n = 19 PBQ studies):
- PBQ validity for **low-complexity jobs**: r = .40
- PBQ validity for **high-complexity jobs**: r = .31
- SQ validity for **low-complexity jobs**: r = .26
- SQ validity for **high-complexity jobs**: r = .23

**Huffcutt et al. 2004** (meta-analysis by question type and complexity):
- PBQs: validity is **relatively stable** across complexity (.30 low vs. .31 high)
- SQs: validity **declines significantly** with complexity (.27 low → .18 high)

**Implication:** For **senior/executive-level roles** (high job complexity), PBQs (STAR-format behavioral questions) maintain their predictive validity while SQs (hypothetical scenarios) degrade. This is the primary evidence-based argument for emphasizing behavioral over situational questions at senior levels.

**Huffcutt, Weekley, Wiesner, DeGroot & Jones 2001** (higher-level positions):
- Behavior description interviews (BDI) outperform situational interviews for higher-level jobs
- For senior roles, PBQs are more valid than SQs regardless of score correction method

### Senior-Level-Specific Design Adjustments

| Feature | Junior/Entry Level | Senior/Executive Level | Evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Primary question type | SQ and PBQ mixed | PBQ/STAR dominant | Huffcutt et al. 2004; Taylor & Small 2002 [S5] |
| Competency breadth | Narrow, task-specific | Strategic, cross-functional | Job complexity literature |
| Experience requirement for PBQ | May use non-work examples | Work examples required | Campion et al. 1994; Gibb & Taylor 2003 |
| Temporal scope of examples | 1–2 years | 3–10 years (breadth of impact) | Practitioner consensus |
| Construct emphasis | Technical skill, learning ability | Leadership, strategic judgment, stakeholder management | Huffcutt et al. 2001 [S6] |
| Interview duration | 30–45 min | 45–75 min | Job complexity × question depth |
| Anchors | Concrete behavioral descriptors | Behavioral + strategic outcome anchors | BARS adaptation for complexity |
| Multiple interviewers | 2 | 3–5 (cross-functional panel) | Reliability; perspective breadth |

**Key research note on job experience requirements for PBQs:** Gibb & Taylor 2003 found that lack of prior job experience does **not** moderate PBQ validity — applicants use nonwork experience effectively. However, at senior levels, the expectation IS that work-domain examples are provided, and probing for organizational scale/scope is appropriate.

**Construct shift with seniority:** Huffcutt et al. 2001 [S6] found that at higher job levels, structured interviews more often assess **applied social skills, leadership, and judgment** vs. **technical knowledge and procedural skills** at lower levels. This aligns with the well-established shift from individual contributor to strategic/relational competencies.

## Synthesis: Design Implications for PIPE's Interview Agent

### Evidence-Based Design Choices

1. **Always use structured behavioral questions (STAR/PBQ format)** — maximum validity (.44–.64 ρ), minimum bias (d ≈ 0 for gender; d ≈ .10 for race with PBQs)

2. **Use BARS for all scoring** — 35% validity improvement over unanchored scales; reduces halo and similarity bias; equalizes rater expertise differences

3. **Include both PBQ and SQ types** — complementary constructs (SQ → job knowledge; PBQ → experience/personality); combined > either alone

4. **6–12 questions is the validated sweet spot** — reliability reaches acceptable levels (.73+) at this range; longer interviews add modest incremental reliability

5. **For senior roles, weight PBQs heavily** — SQ validity declines with job complexity; PBQ validity is robust across levels; senior assessments should probe strategic scope, stakeholder complexity, and organizational impact

6. **Multiple raters when possible** — panel reliability .76 vs. individual .73; diversity of interviewers reduces similarity bias

7. **Minimize rapport-building phase** — first impressions formed in rapport building contaminate subsequent scoring (Barrick et al. 2010)

8. **Rate each question independently before forming an overall impression** — prevents halo effect; post-1996 structured interviews with per-question rating showed near-zero demographic bias (Levashina et al. 2014 [S4])

### Validity Benchmarks for Agent-Scored Interviews

| Method | Expected Validity (ρ) | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Fully structured STAR + BARS | .44–.64 | Matches best-in-class human structured interview |
| SQ-only | .25–.35 | Adequate; lower for senior roles |
| Unstructured / holistic AI score | ~.38 | Equivalent to unstructured human interview |
| GMA test alone | .65 | Not an interview; benchmark comparison |

## Gaps

1. **AI-scored interview validity** — no published meta-analyses as of 2026 specifically on LLM-scored behavioral interviews; the human-interview validity literature provides the ceiling targets but agent performance must be independently validated

2. **Culture-fit construct validity** — limited IO psychology research on what "culture fit" interviews actually measure vs. similarity bias; this dimension needs careful construct definition to avoid similarity bias masquerading as criterion

3. **Seniority-level sample size** — most meta-analyses do not stratify by job level; the high-complexity findings are extrapolated, not directly tested for C-suite roles

4. **Asynchronous/recorded interview validity** — Levashina et al. 2014 [S4] identified this as an understudied area; face-to-face validity (.73) > phone > video in structure utilization

5. **Optimal probing depth** — the research is split on whether probing increases or decreases validity and faking; no consensus exists on optimal follow-up probe design

## Findings by sub-question

### Q1 — JTBD switch interview methodology

The JTBD switch interview, developed by Bob Moesta, Chris Spiek, and grounded in Clayton Christensen's Jobs-to-be-Done theory, is a structured retrospective protocol that reconstructs a specific purchase decision by working backward from the moment of commitment [S4, S5, S50]. The core innovation is recognizing that every purchase is a "switch"—the customer fires an old solution and hires a new one—and the forces that govern that switch reveal the job being done far more reliably than asking "what do you want?" [S5, S6, S7, S49].

The switch interview opens with a moment-anchor question: "Why did you buy [product] and where were you when you bought it?" [S50]. This grounds the conversation in a specific event rather than abstract preference, leveraging the cognitive interview technique from forensic psychology: putting the interviewee back in the mental and emotional context where the decision occurred [S29, S31]. The interviewer then works backward through a six-stage timeline: first thought (initial realization something must change), passive looking (casual noticing without effort), active looking (deliberate research and comparison), deciding (commitment point), onboarding (first experience), and ongoing use (retention or churn) [S32, S33, S49]. The transition between passive and active looking is particularly diagnostic—it reveals what event elevated the problem from "nice to have" to "urgent" [S32, S49].

At each stage, the interviewer probes the four forces diagram developed by Moesta and the Re-Wired Group [S2, S46, S47, S48]. Push represents the struggles or frustrations with the current situation that motivate change. Pull represents the attraction to the new solution's promise. Anxiety represents the fears and uncertainties about adopting something unfamiliar (will I regret this? will it work?). Habit represents the comfort and inertia of the current way, even if imperfect ("it's our mess, but we understand it") [S2, S47]. The decision to switch occurs when push plus pull exceeds anxiety plus habit [S48]. Critically, Gourville's research cited by Rivera [S47] shows that buyers value losses approximately 3x more than gains, while sellers value gains 3x more than losses, creating a potential 9x perception gap between what a seller thinks they're offering and what a buyer experiences. This asymmetry explains why "better" products fail—they aren't 9x better.

The switch interview uses specific memory-enhancement techniques adapted from cognitive psychology [S50, S51]. Environmental cuing involves asking about weather, location, lighting, time of day, and sensory details: "Was it hot or cold? Was it morning? Were you in the office or at home?" These concrete anchors trigger contextual memory far more effectively than abstract timeline questions [S50]. Specificity prompts encourage naming: "What was your spouse's reaction? What's your dog's name?" The granular detail serves two purposes—it improves recall accuracy by activating episodic memory, and it signals to the interviewee that the interviewer genuinely wants to understand their lived experience, not just extract bullet points [S50, S63].

Contrast questions are a signature JTBD probe technique [S29, S50, S62, S63]. Instead of asking "why did you choose X?" the interviewer asks "why X instead of Y?" This forces the interviewee to articulate their decision criteria explicitly. As Moesta notes, providing contrast leads to greater understanding—every vague word hides a specific comparison [S29, S62]. When someone says a product is "fast," the interviewer asks "faster than what?" When they say "reliable," the probe is "reliable compared to what you were using before?" [S62]. This technique surfaces the evaluative dimensions that mattered in the actual decision, which often differ from what people claim matters in abstract preference surveys.

Applying JTBD to hiring reveals a structural insight: the hiring manager is not the job executor; the candidate is [S8, S38]. In Ulwick's Outcome-Driven Innovation terminology, the job executor is the person who actually uses the product to get a job done [S8]. For hiring, this means the new hire is trying to "hire" a new role to do a job in their life (feel respected, feel challenged, learn, grow, ship products they're proud of, achieve work-life balance) [S6, S7, S67, S68]. The hiring manager is hiring the role to do a different job for the team (increase velocity, reduce bugs, mentor juniors, unblock the roadmap). Most intake interviews focus exclusively on the hiring manager's job and ignore the candidate's job entirely, which is why job descriptions fail to attract the right people [S67, S68]. The JTBD lens forces the question: "Why would a candidate leave their current job for this one?" That question inverts the entire intake conversation—suddenly the hiring manager must articulate push (what's broken at other companies that we fix?), pull (what's uniquely compelling here?), anxiety (why would a candidate worry this is the wrong move?), and habit (what makes staying put feel safer?) [S67, S68]. This is the demand-side theory applied to talent acquisition [S6, S7, S67].

The switch interview's applicability to recruiter intake is direct. When a hiring manager says "we need a senior backend engineer with Kafka experience," the JTBD-trained interviewer hears a solution statement, not a job statement. The probe sequence becomes: "When did you first realize you needed that?" (first thought anchor). "Walk me through what happened that day—where were you, what broke, who was in the room?" (cognitive interview + environmental cuing). "Why senior instead of mid-level?" (contrast). "Why Kafka instead of another message queue, or no message queue at all?" (contrast + surfacing the functional job). "What are you worried will happen if we hire someone without Kafka?" (anxiety). "What makes Kafka feel like the safe answer?" (habit). "Why now instead of next quarter?" (transition from passive to active). This line of questioning reconstructs the timeline of the decision to open the role and surfaces the actual job the hiring manager is trying to get done, which is rarely "hire someone who knows Kafka" and far more often "reduce the risk of data loss during traffic spikes" or "unblock the growth team who's been waiting 8 weeks for real-time events."

### Q2 — Demand-side JTBD for hiring: buyer-side interview revealing candidate-side jobs

The demand-side school of JTBD, articulated most clearly by Alan Klement [S6, S7] and Bob Moesta [S29, S30, S31], is grounded in the principle that people don't buy products—they hire products to make progress in their lives [S6, S7]. This theory of progress, not product attributes, is what Klement calls the demand-side lens [S6]. The contrast is supply-side thinking: "here's what our product does, who wants it?" versus demand-side thinking: "here's the progress people are trying to make—what are they currently hiring to make that progress, and why might they fire it?" [S6, S7].

In hiring, the traditional intake interview is pure supply-side: "here's the role we have, what skills do we need to fill it?" The demand-side reframe is: "here's the progress a candidate is trying to make in their career—what are they currently hiring (their current job) to make that progress, and why would they fire it to hire us?" [S67, S68]. This inversion has a critical implication: you cannot answer the demand-side question by interviewing only the hiring manager. You must either interview candidates (people who recently made a job switch) or you must force the hiring manager to empathize with the candidate's perspective deeply enough to reconstruct it [S67, S68].

Moesta's switch interview applied to hiring does exactly this [S67]. The Re-Wired Group has taught workshops on applying JTBD to hiring by interviewing recent hires [S67]. The protocol: find someone who recently joined the company, and ask them to reconstruct the timeline of their decision to leave their previous role. First thought: "When did you first realize you might need to leave?" Passive looking: "What made you start noticing job posts?" Active looking: "What made you start actually applying and interviewing?" Deciding: "What tipped you from 'this is interesting' to 'I'm taking the offer'?" [S32, S67]. The four forces surface the demand-side story [S67, S68]. Push factors for engineers leaving a job: solving the same problem over and over, stagnation (not learning), lack of mentorship, working on products no one uses, working with people who don't care about code quality, toxic culture, no respect [S68]. Pull factors: opportunity to learn new stack, work with senior engineers, ship products that matter, better work-life balance, remote flexibility, higher comp [S68]. Anxiety factors: risk of joining a failing startup, risk of bad manager, risk of being the "only senior" and getting overwhelmed, impostor syndrome [S68]. Habit factors: golden handcuffs (unvested equity), comfort of knowing the codebase and the team, fear of restarting the "proving yourself" cycle [S68].

The key insight for intake design: the hiring manager can surface these demand-side forces if the interviewer probes correctly. When a hiring manager says "we need someone who doesn't need hand-holding," the JTBD-trained intake agent doesn't write down "independent" and move on. The probe is: "Tell me about the last person you hired who did need hand-holding. What did that look like day-to-day? What was the cost to the team? What would have been different if they hadn't needed it?" [INDI-3]. This reconstructs a micro-switch story within the hiring manager's experience, and from that story the agent can infer the candidate-side job: a candidate who takes this role is hiring it to do the job of "feel trusted and autonomous" or "avoid micromanagement." Now the agent has both sides of the market: the hiring manager is trying to hire someone to reduce management overhead, and the candidate is trying to hire a role to gain autonomy. If those align, it's a match. If they conflict (hiring manager wants autonomy but plans to micromanage), it's a hidden mismatch the intake must surface [INDI-3].

The evidence for this approach is stronger in consumer JTBD than in hiring specifically (hiring application is recent and less documented [S67, S68]), but the logic is sound: every hire is a two-sided switch. The candidate fires their current job and hires a new one. The hiring manager fires the status quo (understaffed team, or the previous person in the role) and hires a new person. Both sides experience push, pull, anxiety, and habit. The intake interview that ignores the candidate's four forces is designing a one-sided market, which is why so many hires fail—89% due to motivational and attitudinal mismatch, not technical skill [S78]. The hiring manager thought they were hiring for React skills (supply-side), but the candidate was hiring for "work on products I'm proud of" (demand-side), and the actual product was B2B SaaS CRUD apps. React skills were there, but the job-to-be-done wasn't, so the hire churned [S78].

### Q3 — Outcome-Driven Innovation (Ulwick): does it add anything distinct beyond switch interviews?

Tony Ulwick's Outcome-Driven Innovation (ODI) is the methodological operationalization of JTBD theory [S8, S36]. Ulwick's framing: "If Jobs-to-be-Done is the theory, then Outcome-Driven Innovation is the practice" [S8]. ODI is a structured, quantitative process that transforms qualitative JTBD insights into measurable innovation opportunities with an 86% success rate (5x industry average) [S9].

The core distinction between ODI and Moesta's switch interview methodology is this: Moesta focuses on reconstructing a single decision narrative to understand the forces and timeline of one purchase. Ulwick focuses on systematically mapping all the outcome statements customers use to measure success while executing a job, then quantitatively scoring those outcomes on importance and satisfaction to identify unmet needs [S8, S9, S36, S38, S39]. Both are JTBD methodologies, but they serve different purposes. Switch interviews answer "why did this person switch?" ODI answers "across a population, which dimensions of the job are underserved, and therefore where should we innovate?" [S8, S36].

The outcome statement format is Ulwick's signature contribution [S8, S37, S38, S40]. An outcome statement has four components: (1) direction of improvement (minimize or maximize), (2) a unit of measurement (time, likelihood, number of, amount of), (3) the object of control (the thing being acted upon), and (4) a contextual clarifier (the circumstances under which the job is being done) [S37, S38, S40]. Example from Ulwick: "Minimize the time it takes to determine what nutrition is needed to address the pet's existing health problems" [S38]. Another example: "Minimize the time it takes to get the songs in the desired order for listening" [S37]. The grammatical structure forces clarity: it separates the customer's desired end-state (the outcome) from the means of achieving it (the product feature). "I want fast onboarding" is not an outcome statement—it's vague. "Minimize the number of weeks it takes for a new hire to ship a feature independently, even when the codebase is undocumented" is an outcome statement [ODI-1]. It specifies direction (minimize), metric (weeks), object (time to independent shipping), and context (undocumented codebase).

For recruiter intake, the outcome statement format is a powerful disambiguation tool. When a hiring manager says "we need fast onboarding," the intake agent translates it into an outcome statement by probing: "You want to minimize the number of weeks it takes for a new hire to ship independently?" [ODI-1]. Then the follow-up: "Even when the codebase is undocumented? Even when the team is remote? Even when it's a junior hire?" The contextual clarifier forces the hiring manager to confront the actual constraints of the job [ODI-1]. This is distinct from the JTBD switch interview's strength (narrative reconstruction) but complementary: the switch interview surfaces why the hiring manager cares about fast onboarding (what event triggered urgency? what's the cost of slow onboarding?), while the outcome statement surfaces what "fast onboarding" actually means as a measurable success criterion.

Ulwick's research shows that every functional job has 50+ desired outcome statements [S8, S38]. For hiring, this implies that "hire a senior backend engineer" is not a single job—it's a cluster of 50+ sub-outcomes the hiring manager is trying to achieve, and most intake interviews surface only 3-5 of them [S8]. The ODI intake protocol would be: (1) identify the core functional job ("increase backend team velocity"), (2) map the job into steps (plan sprint, assign tasks, code features, review PRs, deploy, monitor, fix bugs, mentor juniors), (3) for each step, surface 5-10 outcome statements the hiring manager uses to measure success [S8, S38]. Example outcomes for the "review PRs" step: "Minimize the time it takes to spot a security vulnerability in a PR, even when the PR is 2000 lines." "Maximize the likelihood that a junior engineer understands why their PR was rejected, so they don't repeat the mistake." "Minimize the number of review cycles required before a PR is merge-ready." Each outcome reveals a different dimension of what "senior backend engineer" actually means in this context [ODI-1, ODI-2, ODI-3].

The opportunity algorithm—importance + max(importance - satisfaction, 0)—then prioritizes which outcomes are most underserved [S9, S36]. For intake, this quantification is overkill unless you're interviewing dozens of hiring managers across the org, but the conceptual move is valuable: not all outcomes are equally important, and not all important outcomes are equally satisfied by the current team. Asking "on a scale of 1-10, how important is it that a new hire can mentor juniors?" and then "how well does your current team do that?" surfaces the gap [ODI-3]. If mentoring is 9/10 important and the current team is 3/10 capable, that's a high-opportunity outcome (underserved need). If fast coding is 7/10 important and the team is already 8/10 capable, that's low-opportunity (satisfied need, don't over-index) [ODI-3].

The job executor distinction [S8, S38] is another Ulwick contribution relevant to intake. In ODI, the job executor is the person who performs the core functional job [S8]. For "listen to music," the job executor is the listener, not the DJ or the record label. For "hire a backend engineer," the job executor is... the new hire, not the hiring manager [ODI-4]. This is subtle but critical. The hiring manager is a stakeholder in the job (they benefit from it), but they don't execute the job. The new hire executes the job of "be a productive backend engineer on this team." Therefore, the outcomes that matter include both the hiring manager's outcomes (reduce bug rate, increase velocity) and the new hire's outcomes (feel respected, learn new skills, achieve work-life balance). Most intake interviews capture only the hiring manager's outcomes, which is why the EVP pitch later fails—there's no demand-side story for the candidate [ODI-4].

Does ODI add anything distinct beyond switch interviews for intake? Yes: the outcome statement format and the systematic job-mapping process. Switch interviews are deep and narrative-driven; ODI is broad and systematic. For a single intake call, the switch interview's timeline reconstruction is more practical. For synthesizing across many intake calls to build a hiring playbook or a candidate pitch template, ODI's outcome-mapping framework is more scalable. The LLM turn-behavior implication: when the agent hears a vague success criterion ("we need someone who moves fast"), it should translate into an outcome statement ("You want to minimize the number of days between feature request and production deploy?") and then probe for satisfaction ("How fast is your current team? What's the gap?") [ODI-1, ODI-2].

### Q4 — IDEO / d.school empathy — what PIPE is using vs. what IDEO actually prescribes

The current PIPE Role Discovery agent prompt (per the brief's framing) claims to use "IDEO empathy principles," but the gap between invocation and practice is worth diagnosing. IDEO and Stanford d.school's empathy methodology is not a single technique—it's a collection of structured tools designed to force designers past their own assumptions and into the lived experience of users [S10, S11, S12, S13, S14, S15]. The core move is defamiliarization: making the familiar strange so designers question "well-rehearsed habits" and see with fresh eyes [MADSBJERG-3]. The specific tools include empathy maps, journey maps, extreme user interviews, How/Why laddering, and the Five Whys [S10, S11, S12, S13, S14, S15, S42, S43, S44, S45, S69, S70].

The empathy map is a four-quadrant framework: Says, Thinks, Does, Feels [S10, S11, S12]. Says captures verbatim quotes from user research—what the user literally said out loud. Thinks captures what the user is thinking throughout the experience, especially what they think but won't vocalize. Does captures observable behaviors and actions. Feels captures emotional state—worries, excitement, frustration [S10, S11, S12]. The critical insight is the Says/Thinks gap [EMPATHY-1]. What users say and what they actually think often diverge. A hiring manager might say "we value work-life balance," but think "if they don't reply to Slack on weekends, they're not committed" [EMPATHY-1]. The empathy map externalizes this contradiction so the team can see it [S10]. The d.school's guidance: "pay special attention to what users think, but may not be willing to vocalize" [S10]. For intake, this means the LLM agent must probe past the hiring manager's public script ("we're a great place to work") to the private anxiety ("I'm worried we'll hire another person who quits after 6 months because they can't handle the hours") [EMPATHY-1].

Empathy mapping translates into design decisions by revealing three types of insight: contradictions (Says/Does mismatch, or Says/Thinks mismatch), unmet needs the user doesn't explicitly voice, and biases the design team holds that aren't grounded in user reality [S10]. For intake, the contradictions are gold. When a hiring manager says "we need a team player" (Says) but you probe and discover they fired the last person for pushing back on bad ideas (Does), that's a Says/Does contradiction that reveals the real culture [EMPATHY-2]. The LLM turn behavior: when you hear a value statement, probe for the story that contradicts it. "You said you value work-life balance. Tell me about the last time someone on the team took a full week off. What happened?" [EMPATHY-2].

The d.school's How/Why laddering technique is a structured interview tool for moving between abstraction levels [S44, S45]. Starting with a surface-level need, the interviewer repeatedly asks "why?" to climb toward abstract, emotional, philosophical needs (why does that matter? why is that important? why do you care?). Then, from any node on the ladder, the interviewer can ask "how?" to descend toward concrete, actionable specifics (how would you do that? how do you know it worked? how did it feel?) [S44]. The d.school's guidance: "the kinds of responses from this exercise tend to go deep and be much more philosophical, emotional and social" [S44]. Example for hiring: "We need a senior engineer" (surface). "Why senior?" "To mentor juniors." "Why is mentoring important?" "Because turnover is killing us." "Why is turnover happening?" "Because juniors feel unsupported and leave." Now you're at the real job: reduce turnover by increasing support [DSCHOOL-1]. The ladder climbed from "senior engineer" (solution/feature) to "reduce turnover by increasing support" (emotional/systemic need). You can also descend: "How do juniors currently get support?" "They don't." "How do you know they're unsupported?" "Three juniors quit in six months and all said the same thing in exit interviews." Now you have thick description [DSCHOOL-1].

The Five Whys, as practiced by the d.school (not to be confused with the lean manufacturing version), is a related technique: repeatedly ask why a problem occurs to surface root causes not immediately apparent [S13, S69, S70]. The d.school's framing: this moves from symptom to systemic issue [S13]. Example: "We need Kubernetes experience." "Why?" "Deployments are broken." "Why are they broken?" "No one understands the YAML." "Why doesn't anyone understand it?" "We have no documentation and the one person who knew it left." "Why did they leave?" "We don't pay enough to retain senior people." Now you know the root cause isn't a Kubernetes skill gap—it's a retention and documentation problem [DSCHOOL-4]. The LLM turn behavior: when you hear a solution ("we need X skill"), ladder down with five whys to find the root problem [DSCHOOL-4].

Extreme users is another d.school technique [S13]. Instead of interviewing the median user, interview the edges: power users, non-users, frustrated users, delighted users. The d.school's rationale: "needs are amplified and workarounds are more notable" at the extremes, and insights from extreme users often apply to the broader population [S13, DSCHOOL-3]. For intake: ask the hiring manager "who on your team is most frustrated with the current skill gap? Can I talk to them?" and "who adapted best to the current constraints? What do they do differently?" [DSCHOOL-3]. The extremes reveal the range of coping strategies and unmet needs that the median hides.

Journey mapping (IDEO) visualizes the user's process over time, including physical, digital, and human touchpoints at each stage [S42, S43]. Critically, a good journey map includes mindsets (thoughts, questions, motivations) and emotions (plotted as a line showing ups and downs) at each stage [S42, S43]. For intake: "Walk me through the last time you hired for this role. First thought → posted on LinkedIn → screened resumes → first-round interviews → final interviews → offer → onboarding. At each stage, what broke? Where did you lose good candidates? Where did you waste time?" [JOURNEY-1]. The journey map reveals process failures the hiring manager might not connect (e.g., "we lost our best candidate between round 2 and round 3 because it took us 3 weeks to schedule round 3" is a journey-map insight) [JOURNEY-1].

The d.school's empathy interview best practices [S13, S14, S15, S69, S70, S71]: (1) become a "Why machine"—be truly curious, (2) encourage storytelling, (3) ask for concrete examples, not hypotheticals, (4) use open-ended, non-binary questions, (5) avoid leading questions, (6) be present and attentive (don't be distracted by note-taking—record it), (7) the goal is to discover gaps between what people do and what people say they do [S13, S69, S70, DSCHOOL-2]. The framing "gaps between what people do and say they do" is the d.school's version of the empathy map's Says/Does contradiction [DSCHOOL-2].

What is PIPE likely using versus what IDEO prescribes? If the PIPE agent is doing shallow "what do you need?" questions and stopping at the first answer, it's not using IDEO empathy—it's using a survey. IDEO empathy requires probing contradictions, asking why repeatedly, surfacing stories, and challenging assumptions [S10, S11, S12, S13, S69, S70]. The gap is the difference between "what skills do you need?" (survey) and "tell me about the last person who failed in this role—what happened, how did it feel, what would have prevented it?" (empathy interview) [DSCHOOL-2]. The latter surfaces Says/Thinks/Does/Feels. The former surfaces a checklist.

### Q5 — Madsbjerg Sensemaking: thick data, phenomenology, defamiliarization

Christian Madsbjerg, co-founder of ReD Associates, developed the sensemaking methodology as a critique of "thin data" approaches that reduce human behavior to quantifiable patterns without cultural or phenomenological depth [S16, S17, S18]. Madsbjerg's core argument: "companies must understand the human beings represented in their data sets" or risk losing market relevance [S17]. Sensemaking is grounded in phenomenology, the philosophical study of lived experience as it actually is, not as we think it should be [S16, S17, S18]. The approach uses thick description (Clifford Geertz) [S72, S73, S74, S75], defamiliarization [S17, S18, S74], and ethnographic immersion to produce "thick data"—data that captures texture, intentionality, cultural meaning, and emotional resonance [S16, S17, S18].

Phenomenology, in Madsbjerg's hands, is a directive to describe phenomena without imposing preconceived frameworks [S16, S17, S18]. ReD Associates asks clients to reframe questions phenomenologically: not "how do we sell more toys?" but "why do kids play?" [S18]. The shift moves from solution-space (sell more) to experience-space (play). For intake: not "what skills do we need to hire?" but "what does it feel like to be a backend engineer on this team?" [MADSBJERG-1]. The phenomenological question forces the hiring manager to describe the lived experience, which surfaces cultural and emotional dimensions a skills checklist erases.

Thick description, borrowed from anthropologist Clifford Geertz [S72, S73, S74, S75], is the practice of capturing not just observable behavior but the intentional, communicative, interpretative meaning of the behavior: why it was done, how it was read, using which social codes [S72, S73, MADSBJERG-2]. Geertz's famous example: a wink versus a twitch. Observable behavior: eyelid closes. Thin description: "eyelid closed." Thick description: "he winked at his co-conspirator to signal the plan was on, and she smirked because she knew he was bluffing, and the boss didn't notice because he was checking his phone" [S72, S73]. Thick description embeds the action in social context, intentionality, and cultural meaning [S72, S73, S74, S75].

For intake, thin description is "we have daily standups." Thick description is "we have daily standups at 9am, and the backend team is in three time zones, so the engineer in Berlin is on at 3pm and the engineer in San Francisco is on at 6am, and the Berlin engineer talks for 10 minutes about what he did yesterday because he's German and thorough, and the SF engineer says 'no blockers' every day because she's exhausted and resentful, and the product manager doesn't notice because she's multitasking, and the whole thing feels like theater not problem-solving" [MADSBJERG-2]. The thick description reveals cultural dynamics (German thoroughness vs. American brevity), power dynamics (PM not listening), timezone dysfunction, and emotional undercurrents (resentment). None of that appears in "we have daily standups" [MADSBJERG-2]. The LLM turn behavior: when the hiring manager names a process or practice, probe for thick description: "What happens in standup? Who talks? Who's silent? When does it feel useful versus theater?" [MADSBJERG-2].

Defamiliarization is the ethnographic technique of making the familiar strange to surface assumptions [S17, S18, S74]. Madsbjerg and ReD Associates use defamiliarization to "question well-rehearsed habits" and escape "default thinking" [S17, S18]. The classic defamiliarization prompt: "If an outsider saw this, what would confuse them? What would they think is weird that you think is normal?" [MADSBJERG-3]. For intake: "If a new hire joined your team tomorrow and saw your Slack channels, what would confuse them? What would they think is normal that actually isn't?" [MADSBJERG-3]. This prompt forces the hiring manager to see their culture from the outside, which surfaces unspoken norms, dysfunctions, and tribal knowledge that onboarding assumes but never teaches.

Madsbjerg's critique of algorithmic empathy is relevant to the LLM intake agent design [S16, S17, S18]. Algorithmic empathy is the belief that you can understand humans by analyzing behavioral data patterns at scale without qualitative immersion. Madsbjerg argues this produces thin data: you know what people do, but not why, not how it feels, not what it means in their cultural context [S16, S17, S18]. For the LLM agent: if the agent is pattern-matching ("you said 'fast,' so I'll ask about velocity metrics"), it's doing algorithmic empathy. If the agent is probing for phenomenology ("walk me through a day in the life of your best engineer—what do they do at 10am, what pisses them off, what makes them proud?"), it's doing thick-data sensemaking [MADSBJERG-1]. The difference is the agent's listening mode: thin listening extracts keywords and entities; thick listening absorbs narrative, context, and emotional texture [INDI-1, MADSBJERG-1].

Does Madsbjerg's sensemaking have implications for how the LLM agent should listen? Yes, but they're more philosophical than procedural. The implication is a stance: the agent should treat the hiring manager as a cultural informant describing a lived world, not as a requirements specifier listing features. The agent should probe for texture, contradiction, ritual, meaning, and felt experience, not just extract nouns and verbs. The agent should defamiliarize the hiring manager's defaults ("you said 'we're a meritocracy'—what does that actually mean here? Can you give me an example of how merit gets evaluated?") [MADSBJERG-3]. This is less a specific turn-behavior rule and more a meta-rule: prioritize thick over thin, phenomenology over abstraction, story over summary [MADSBJERG-1, MADSBJERG-2, MADSBJERG-3].

### Q6 — Narrative transportation: what makes a story retellable and persuasive

Green and Brock's narrative transportation theory (2000) [S19, S20] is the foundational psychology of how stories persuade. Transportation is defined as absorption into a story, involving imagery, affect, and attentional focus [S19, S20, S52, S53]. When a listener is transported, they mentally enter the world the story evokes, and in that transported state, they are less likely to counterargue, more likely to emotionally engage, and more likely to adopt beliefs implied by the narrative [S19, S52, S53, NARRATIVE-1]. Transportation persuades differently than argument: argument triggers critical thinking and counterarguing, while narrative triggers immersion and experiential simulation [S19, S52, NARRATIVE-1].

The Transportation-Imagery Model (Green & Brock 2002, expanded in Green & Appel 2024) [S19, S52, S53] identifies three core components of transportation: (1) mental imagery—the listener forms vivid mental pictures of the story events, (2) emotional engagement—strong emotional response with empathy for characters, (3) focused attention—the listener becomes so absorbed they lose awareness of their surroundings [S19, S52, S53]. All three must be present for full transportation [S52, S53]. The persuasive mechanisms are well-documented [S19, S52, S53, NARRATIVE-5]: reduced counterarguing (the transported listener doesn't interrupt enjoyment to argue with the story's implications), increased perceived realism (vivid imagery makes the story feel real even if fictional), emotional engagement (empathy for characters creates emotional investment in outcomes), and mental simulation (the listener rehearses the story's events in their own memory, making it feel like a personal experience) [S19, S52, S53, NARRATIVE-5].

What story properties increase transportation? Van Laer et al.'s 2014 meta-analysis [S22] synthesized 132 effect sizes across 76 studies and identified key antecedents [S22, NARRATIVE-4]: identifiable characters (the listener can see themselves in or relate to the protagonist), plot structure (clear beginning/middle/end with rising tension and resolution), concrete sensory detail (descriptions that evoke sight, sound, smell, touch, taste), emotional intensity (stakes that matter, conflicts that resonate), and perceived realism (even if fictional, the story must feel plausible in its internal logic) [S22, NARRATIVE-4]. Critically, abstract descriptions and generic characters reduce transportation—the more specific and concrete, the more transporting [S19, S22, S52, S53].

Escalas (2004) [S21] applied narrative transportation to consumer behavior and found that narrative processing creates self-brand connections when consumers map the brand's story onto their own memory [S21, NARRATIVE-3]. The mechanism: when you hear a story, you don't just understand it abstractly—you simulate it by retrieving similar episodes from your own life [S21]. If the story is concrete and vivid enough, it activates your autobiographical memory, and the brand becomes entangled with your self-concept [S21]. For hiring: if the intake agent surfaces a concrete story about the team ("the week before the acquisition, the backend team shipped real-time notifications in 72 hours, and the CEO called them heroes"), and that story is retold to a candidate, the candidate simulates it ("could I do that? have I done something like that? how would it feel?") [NARRATIVE-3]. If the simulation feels good, the candidate forms a self-role connection: "I can see myself there" [S21, NARRATIVE-3].

The research consensus: stories must be concrete, not abstract [S19, S52, S53, NARRATIVE-2]. Green & Brock (2000) [S19]: "stories containing rich, concrete imagery can increase levels of transportation." Green & Appel (2024) [S53]: "using rich and descriptive language that appeals to the senses helps the audience create mental images of the story world and events, a key component of transportation." Concrete detail anchors the story in the listener's sensory imagination, which makes it vivid, memorable, and persuasive [S19, S52, S53, NARRATIVE-2]. Abstract language ("the deploy was stressful") doesn't transport. Concrete language ("I was sitting in the SF office at 2am watching the error rate climb on the Grafana dashboard, and my manager was pacing behind me asking 'can we rollback?' and I said 'if we rollback we lose 6 hours of transactions,' and my hands were shaking on the keyboard") transports [NARRATIVE-2]. The difference is sensory specificity: you can see the Grafana dashboard, hear the manager's voice, feel the keyboard [NARRATIVE-2].

For intake, the implication is clear: the artifact the agent produces must contain at least one concrete, vivid, character-driven story that a candidate can simulate and emotionally engage with [NARRATIVE-1, NARRATIVE-2, NARRATIVE-3]. The intake agent's job is to extract that story from the hiring manager by probing for sensory detail, character (who was there? what did they say? how did they react?), stakes (what was at risk? what would have happened if it failed?), and resolution (what happened? how did it feel?) [NARRATIVE-2, NARRATIVE-4]. The turn behavior: when the hiring manager says something emotionally charged ("it was a really hard week" or "she was amazing"), probe for the story: "Tell me about that week. Where were you? What happened first? Who else was there? What did she do that was amazing?" [NARRATIVE-2]. The goal is to collect enough concrete detail that the story can be retold to a candidate and create transportation [NARRATIVE-1, NARRATIVE-2, NARRATIVE-3].

Stories are 22x more memorable than facts [S64, S65, S66, SALES-1]. This statistic, widely cited in sales and employer branding research [S64, S66, SALES-1, SALES-2], underscores the practical value: if the candidate is interviewing at 5 companies, they will remember the one that told them a story, not the one that listed benefits [S64, S66, SALES-1]. The story must be authentic (real employee experiences, not marketing copy [S66, SALES-2]) and it must be concrete (not "we value innovation" but "last quarter, an engineer pitched a crazy idea in a retro, the CTO said 'try it,' she spent 2 weeks in a cave building it, and it's now our most-used feature") [S66, SALES-2].

### Q7 — Past-the-checklist persona techniques: forcing rich personas from hiring managers

The persona failure mode in hiring is well-documented in recruitment literature [S58, S59, S60, S61, S76, S77, S78, PERSONA-1]. When asked "who is the ideal candidate?" hiring managers default to checklists: "Kafka, 5 years, bachelor's degree, team player" [S76, S77, PERSONA-1]. This is Cooper's "inmates running the asylum" applied to recruiting [S23, S24, COOPER-3]: the hiring manager is designing for technical capability (supply-side) instead of human goals and context (demand-side). The checklist is a symptom of shallow thinking—it's easier to list tools than to describe a person, and it's safer to list credentials than to articulate culture fit [S76, S77, S78].

The cost of checklist hiring is high. Leadership IQ's landmark study [S78]: 46% of new hires fail within 18 months, and only 11% of those failures are due to technical skill gaps. 89% fail due to motivational problems, lack of coachability, and attitudinal issues (culture/values mismatch) [S78, PERSONA-2]. CareerBuilder survey [S78]: 74% of bad hires trace to poor culture fit. The U.S. Department of Labor [S78]: hiring the wrong person costs 30% of their annual package. Yet most intake interviews focus almost entirely on technical skills (the 11%) and barely probe motivation and culture (the 89%) [S78, PERSONA-2]. The checklist is not just shallow—it's actively counterproductive.

Cooper's goal-directed design methodology [S23, S24, S25, S56, S57] offers a corrective. Cooper's directive: "define one specific user for your product; invent a persona—give that user a name and an environment and derive his or her goals" [S23, COOPER-1]. A persona is not a demographic profile (age, gender, job title)—it's a composite portrait with name, picture, job description, goals, motivations, frustrations, and often a quote [S23, S24, S56, COOPER-1]. The persona must be specific enough that you can design for one person instead of trying to please everyone [S23, COOPER-1]. Cooper's research: "you will have far greater success designing for one single person than creating a watered-down product intended to fit all possible people" [S24, COOPER-1].

The "day in the life" context scenario is Cooper's primary tool for forcing specificity [S23, S56, S57, COOPER-2]. In the requirements definition stage, Cooper's teams write context scenarios describing a day in the life of the persona: what they do at 9am, what frustrates them at 11am, what they celebrate at 3pm, how they feel at 5pm [S56, S57, COOPER-2]. The scenario is narrative, not abstract: it's a story with characters, settings, stakes, and emotional beats [S56, COOPER-2]. The scenario forces the designer (or hiring manager) to think concretely: if I can't describe what this person does on Tuesday, I don't actually know who they are [COOPER-2].

For intake, the LLM turn behavior is to refuse the checklist and demand the persona [COOPER-1, PERSONA-1]. When the hiring manager says "React, 5 years, team player," the agent interrupts: "I heard the requirements. Now tell me about a specific person—real or imagined—who you'd be thrilled to hire. What's their story? What are they trying to achieve in their career? What frustrates them about their current job? What makes them excited to get out of bed in the morning?" [PERSONA-1]. If the hiring manager can't answer, the agent prompts: "Think about the best person you've ever worked with in this role. Who were they? What made them great? Describe a day in their life" [PERSONA-1, COOPER-1]. The goal is to extract a persona rich enough to be a character in a story, not a row in a spreadsheet [COOPER-1, COOPER-2].

Recruitment intake best practices (2025 sources [S58, S59, S60, S61]) converge on a similar technique: replace "what skills do you need?" with "what would success look like in 30/90/360 days?" [S60, S61, PERSONA-3]. This question forces the hiring manager to imagine the outcome, which requires imagining the person. "In 90 days, what has this person built/fixed/shipped that makes you say 'we nailed this hire'?" [S60, PERSONA-3]. The 90-day question is a narrative anchor—it situates the persona in a future story with stakes and measurable outcomes. This is distinct from "5 years of React" because it's outcome-focused (what will they do?) rather than credential-focused (what have they done?) [PERSONA-3].

Indi Young's listening session technique [S26, S27, S28, INDI-1] offers another de-checklisting move: start the intake with a single germinal question and let the hiring manager guide. "Tell me about the problem that made you open this role" [INDI-1]. Then the agent shuts up and absorbs. Young's guidance: "avoid biasing your data by letting your participant do all the talking and letting them guide the conversation and direct the order of topics" [INDI-1]. "Turn off your own thinking and be like cotton absorbing" [S28, INDI-1]. The checklist is often a defense mechanism—the hiring manager is nervous or rushed, so they shortcut to bullet points. The germinal question invites narrative instead of interrogation, and narrative reveals persona [INDI-1].

The empathy map's Says/Thinks distinction [S10, EMPATHY-1] is another de-checklisting tool. When the hiring manager says "team player," probe the Thinks layer: "What do you worry will happen if we hire someone who isn't a team player? What did the last solo contributor break?" [EMPATHY-1]. The worry reveals the real need, which is almost never "team player" (a vague virtue) and almost always something specific like "someone who documents their work so others can pick it up" or "someone who doesn't hoard knowledge" [EMPATHY-1]. The specific fear surfaces the persona trait that actually matters.

The d.school's extreme users method [S13, DSCHOOL-3] also breaks checklists: "Who on your team is most frustrated with the current skill gap? Can I talk to them?" This surfaces the persona through a real person's lived experience, not the hiring manager's abstraction [DSCHOOL-3].

The synthesis: checklist hiring is a well-documented failure mode [S76, S77, S78, PERSONA-1, PERSONA-2], and design-thinking / JTBD methods converge on the same correctives: force narrative, force specificity, force persona-with-goals, force day-in-the-life, force outcome-over-credential [COOPER-1, COOPER-2, PERSONA-1, PERSONA-3, INDI-1, DSCHOOL-3]. The LLM agent must be programmed to resist the checklist and probe until it has a character rich enough to retell [COOPER-1, COOPER-2, PERSONA-1].

### Q8 — Turn-behavior rules (PRIMARY DELIVERABLE)

These are testable, specific behavioral rules grounded in the source evidence, formatted as directives the LLM agent must follow during intake turns.

**Rule 1: Anchor every topic with a moment, not an abstraction** [JTBD-3, MOESTA-1, RECALL-1]  
When the hiring manager names a need ("we need senior engineers"), do not ask "why do you need that?" (abstract). Ask "when did you first realize you needed that? Walk me through what happened that day—where were you, what broke, who was in the room?" (moment-anchored). Memory reconstructs from episodic anchors, not timelines.

**Rule 2: Probe all four forces for every stated need** [JTBD-2, JTBD-6]  
After hearing a requirement, probe: (1) Push: "What's broken today? What's the cost of staying the same?" (2) Pull: "What becomes possible if we solve this?" (3) Anxiety: "What worries you about making this change?" (4) Habit: "What makes the current way feel safe or familiar?" All four forces must surface or the decision context is incomplete.

**Rule 3: Never accept a noun without probing for the adjacent verb** [ODI-1, ODI-2, CONTRAST-1]  
When you hear a tool, skill, or credential (Kafka, React, 5 years, senior), immediately ask: "What do you use [X] to do?" or "Why [X] instead of [alternative]?" The verb (the functional job) is the actual requirement; the noun is a solution hypothesis.

**Rule 4: Translate vague success criteria into outcome statements** [ODI-1, ODI-2]  
When the hiring manager says "fast onboarding" or "high quality" or "team player," translate to Ulwick outcome format: "You want to minimize the [metric: weeks/bugs/conflicts] it takes to [verb: ship/integrate/collaborate], even when [context: codebase is undocumented / team is remote]?" Then confirm or refine.

**Rule 5: Contrast is mandatory for every choice** [JTBD-5, MOESTA-2, CONTRAST-1]  
Every preference implies an alternative. Probe the contrast: "Why React instead of Vue?" "Why senior instead of mid-level?" "Why hire now instead of Q3?" "Why backfill instead of reorganize?" The contrast forces articulation of decision criteria that "why do you want X?" misses.

**Rule 6: Probe the Says/Thinks gap when you hear a virtue or value** [EMPATHY-1, EMPATHY-2]  
When the hiring manager Says "we value work-life balance" or "we're a meritocracy" or "we want a team player," probe for Thinks: "What do you worry will happen if we hire someone who doesn't fit that? Tell me about the last time that value was tested—what happened?" The fear or story reveals what the value actually means in practice.

**Rule 7: Ladder up with 'why' to find root cause; ladder down with 'how' for specificity** [DSCHOOL-1, DSCHOOL-4]  
Use How/Why laddering and Five Whys. When given a surface need ("we need Kubernetes"), ladder up: "Why Kubernetes?" → "Deploys are broken." → "Why are they broken?" → "No one understands YAML." → "Why?" → Root cause: documentation or retention problem, not skill gap. Then ladder down: "How do deploys break? How often? How much does it cost?"

**Rule 8: Demand a day-in-the-life scenario before ending the call** [COOPER-2, PERSONA-3]  
Do not accept a checklist. Before the intake ends, the agent must have extracted: "Walk me through Tuesday for your ideal hire. 9am standup—what do they say? 2pm code review—what do they catch? 5pm—what do they ship?" If the hiring manager can't describe a day, they don't have a persona—they have a wishlist.

**Rule 9: Probe for sensory/concrete detail in every story** [NARRATIVE-2, JTBD-4, MADSBJERG-2]  
When the hiring manager offers an emotionally charged statement ("it was a disaster" / "she was amazing" / "we barely survived"), probe for thick description: "What did you see? What did [person] say? Where were you sitting? What time of day was it? How did it feel?" Concrete beats abstract for transportation and retellability.

**Rule 10: When you hear a process or practice, probe for thick description** [MADSBJERG-2]  
"We have daily standups" is thin. Probe: "What happens in standup? Who talks? Who's silent? When does it feel useful versus performative?" "We use agile" → "What does agile mean here? Walk me through your last sprint retro." Thick description surfaces culture; thin description surfaces jargon.

**Rule 11: Defamiliarize the familiar to surface assumptions** [MADSBJERG-3]  
Ask: "If a new hire saw your team's Slack tomorrow, what would confuse them? What would they think is normal that actually isn't?" Or: "You said you're a meritocracy—what does merit look like here? Can you give me an example of how someone earned recognition?" Defamiliarization breaks "well-rehearsed habits."

**Rule 12: Flip to demand-side: ask why a candidate would switch TO you** [JTBD-6]  
After probing the hiring manager's needs (supply-side), flip: "Why would a great engineer leave their current job for this one? What's broken at other companies that you fix? What pull do you have?" This forces the hiring manager to empathize with candidate demand-side forces and surfaces the EVP story.

**Rule 13: Probe the passive → active transition to reveal urgency** [JTBD-7]  
"When did you start thinking about hiring?" (passive looking). "What made you actually post the role / start interviewing?" (active looking). The event that triggers the transition reveals priority, budget unlock, and timeline pressure.

**Rule 14: Use non-directed listening at the start; structured probes after** [INDI-1, INDI-3]  
Open with a germinal question: "Tell me about the problem that made you open this role." Let the hiring manager talk uninterrupted for 2-3 minutes. Absorb. Then probe gaps with structured questions (forces, contrast, laddering, thick description). Early interruption biases the data.

**Rule 15: Identify the job executor (candidate) vs. stakeholder (hiring manager)** [ODI-4, JTBD-6]  
Probe both: "What job is the new hire trying to do in their career?" (demand-side) vs. "What job are you hiring this role to do for the team?" (supply-side). The gap between them is the culture-fit risk. If the hiring manager can only answer the second, probe the first until they empathize.

**Rule 16: Replace "Do you need X?" with "Tell me about the last time X was missing"** [DSCHOOL-2]  
Leading questions ("Do you need leadership skills?") bias toward yes. Story-based questions ("Tell me about the last time a project failed because of a people problem—what happened?") surface unbiased reality.

## Synthesis

The design-thinking and Jobs-to-Be-Done traditions converge on a unified critique of shallow intake: checklists, abstractions, and feature-lists fail because they erase context, motivation, emotion, and narrative [S23, S24, S76, S77, S78, COOPER-3, PERSONA-1, PERSONA-2]. The correctives are structural, not cosmetic. JTBD's switch interview and four forces [S4, S5, S46, S47, S48, JTBD-1, JTBD-2] force reconstruction of a decision timeline and the emotional/functional forces that drove it. Ulwick's ODI [S8, S36, S37, S38, ODI-1, ODI-2] disambiguates vague needs into measurable outcome statements. IDEO and d.school empathy methods [S10, S11, S12, S13, S44, S69, S70, EMPATHY-1, DSCHOOL-1, DSCHOOL-2] surface the Says/Thinks/Does/Feels gaps and use How/Why laddering to move between abstraction and specificity. Madsbjerg's sensemaking [S16, S17, S18, S72, S73, MADSBJERG-1, MADSBJERG-2, MADSBJERG-3] insists on thick description and phenomenology over thin behavioral data. Green & Brock's transportation theory [S19, S20, S52, S53, NARRATIVE-1, NARRATIVE-2] reveals why the artifact must contain a concrete, sensory, character-driven story to persuade candidates later. Cooper's goal-directed design [S23, S24, S56, S57, COOPER-1, COOPER-2] refuses personas-as-checklists and demands day-in-the-life scenarios.

The three biggest design-thinking moves PIPE's current agent is likely missing:

First, **the agent is probably not probing contradictions**. The Says/Thinks gap [EMPATHY-1], the Says/Does gap [EMPATHY-2], and the supply/demand mismatch [JTBD-6, ODI-4] are where insight lives, but surfacing contradictions requires the agent to challenge the hiring manager ("you said work-life balance, but you also said weekends—help me understand"). Most LLM agents are conflict-averse and will accept the first answer. Design thinking demands defamiliarization and probing discomfort [MADSBJERG-3, EMPATHY-2].

Second, **the agent is probably not demanding stories**. If the artifact contains "we need 5 years of React experience," it has failed. The artifact must contain a day-in-the-life scenario [COOPER-2], a switch-moment narrative [JTBD-1, JTBD-3], and at least one concrete, sensory, emotionally charged story with a character the candidate can identify with [NARRATIVE-1, NARRATIVE-2, NARRATIVE-3, NARRATIVE-4]. Extracting that requires the agent to probe: "Tell me about the week that made you realize you need this role. What happened? Who was there? What did it feel like?" [NARRATIVE-2, MOESTA-1]. The story is the sales pitch; the checklist is not.

Third, **the agent is probably not translating nouns into verbs**. When the hiring manager says "Kafka," the agent writes down "Kafka" and moves on. The ODI and JTBD methods insist: Kafka is a solution hypothesis, not a need. The need is the outcome: "minimize message loss during traffic spikes" or "maximize real-time event throughput for the growth team" [ODI-1, ODI-2]. The verb (the functional job) is what the agent must surface, because the verb transfers to the scorecard (can this candidate do the verb?) and the sales pitch (this role lets you do the verb) in ways the noun never does. Translating nouns to verbs requires relentless contrast probing: "Why Kafka instead of X? What does Kafka let you do that you can't do otherwise?" [JTBD-5, ODI-2, CONTRAST-1].

Adding these three moves changes what lands in the artifact from a 2D list (skills, years, traits) to a 3D world (a team with culture, a problem with stakes, a day with rhythm, a candidate with goals, a story with transportation). The 3D artifact serves both downstream consumers: the hiring scorecard (now we know what outcomes to test for, not just what keywords to grep) and the candidate pitch (now we have a story that transports, not a job description that lists).

## Gaps

These methods are silent on several dimensions the Lead must handle:

**Quantification and prioritization.** JTBD and design thinking excel at surfacing needs, but they don't prescribe how to prioritize conflicting needs or quantify trade-offs. Ulwick's ODI does (importance/satisfaction scoring [S9, S36, ODI-3]), but that requires surveying many stakeholders, not one intake call. The Lead must decide: if the hiring manager surfaces 15 outcomes, which 5 go into the scorecard?

**Multi-stakeholder synthesis.** The brief mentions multi-person interviews. These methods (especially Cooper, IDEO, Indi Young) assume you're synthesizing across many users to build a composite persona or mental model tower [S54, INDI-2]. One intake call with one hiring manager produces one perspective. If the Lead interviews the hiring manager, the recruiter, and two engineers on the team, how do you resolve contradictions? None of these frameworks explicitly address that, though Indi Young's mental model towers [S54, INDI-2] are designed for multi-participant synthesis (patterns emerge across towers).

**LLM-specific challenges.** These are human-interviewer methods. Adapting them to an LLM agent introduces challenges the literature doesn't cover: How does the agent know when it has "enough" thick description? How does it recognize a Says/Thinks contradiction if the hiring manager doesn't explicitly state both (humans infer from tone and body language; LLMs can't)? How does it balance structured probing (Rule 1-16) with Indi Young's "be like cotton" non-directive listening [INDI-1]—those pull in opposite directions. The Lead must operationalize these tensions.

**Legal and compliance boundaries.** None of these methods address what the agent must NOT ask (protected-class questions, illegal interview topics). The brief explicitly scopes those out to prior research, but the turn-behavior rules must integrate compliance guardrails the design-thinking literature ignores.

**The artifact format.** These methods tell you what to extract (stories, outcomes, personas, thick description, forces), but they don't prescribe the output schema. What fields go into the artifact? How is it structured for the scorecard vs. the sales pitch? That's a Lead decision informed by but not answered by this research.

## Question

Describe a commitment you made at work that turned out to be harder than you expected. What did you do when you realized it was going to be hard?

## Why we ask this

Ownership under friction. Anyone can own work when it's easy. The interesting signal is what the candidate does when a commitment they made starts looking expensive — do they renegotiate early and honestly, grind through, or quietly let it slip?

This question is particularly useful for senior and lead roles where the cost of a broken commitment falls on other people, not just the candidate.

## BARS rubric

- **5** — Describes a specific commitment, the moment they realized it would be harder than expected, and a deliberate decision about how to respond. The response shows communication with stakeholders *before* the deadline, not after. Owns the original estimation error. Includes what they learned about their own estimation.
- **4** — Specific commitment and clear response. Communication with stakeholders, though maybe slightly late. Honest about the estimation miss.
- **3** — Describes the commitment but framing suggests they mostly ground through it without renegotiation. Outcome was OK but through brute force.
- **2** — Describes a commitment they missed, without clear accountability. Blames changing requirements or team dependencies.
- **1** — Cannot produce an example. Or: describes a commitment they kept "perfectly" with no struggle (fails the specificity check). Or: describes quietly letting something slip without telling anyone.

## Calibration examples

### Low (1-2)

> "I always deliver on my commitments, so I can't really think of one. Maybe early in my career there was something, but I learned how to scope well."

Evasion via humble-brag. No specificity, no admission of estimation miss. Scores 1.

### Medium (3)

> "I promised to have a dashboard done by end of quarter and the data pipeline was a lot messier than I thought. I ended up working late a few weeks and got it in. Learned to scope better next time."

Brute force, no renegotiation, vague lesson. Scores 3.

### High (4-5)

> "I told our product lead I could ship the new billing migration in three weeks. Week one I realized the historical data was in a worse state than the schema suggested — probably six weeks of real work. I went to her on Wednesday of week two and walked her through what I'd found, gave her three options: slip the date by three weeks, cut the historical backfill and do it separately, or bring in a second engineer. She picked option two. I'm fairly sure the right call would have been to flag it on the Friday of week one, not the Wednesday of week two — I waited because I wanted to be sure. Now when my gut says 'this is worse than I estimated,' I raise it within 48 hours."

Specific commitment, concrete scoping error, early (but honest about "not early enough") renegotiation, options offered to stakeholder, durable change. Scores 5.

## Probe library

- *no renegotiation*: "When did you tell the people depending on this that it would be harder? How did that conversation go?"
- *vague lesson*: "What specifically do you do differently now?"
- *blames others*: "Set aside the changing requirements — what was your part in how the estimate was wrong?"
- *humble-brag evasion*: "Let me rephrase: pick the time that comes closest. What was the situation and what made it hard?"

## Question

Tell me about a time you had to work with another team to get something done, and you didn't have authority over anyone on that other team. What did you do to get alignment?

## Why we ask this

Cross-team work without authority is where senior engineers prove they can be multipliers outside their team. It requires creating shared understanding, managing competing priorities, and accepting that you cannot force anything. Candidates who struggle here tend to either escalate early or work around the other team.

Weaker candidates describe these situations as "getting the other team to do X." Stronger candidates describe them as "finding a version of X that worked for both teams."

## BARS rubric

- **5** — Specific cross-team collaboration with a named goal. Candidate describes the other team's priorities fairly (not as obstacles). Clear evidence of them adapting their ask to fit the other team's constraints — not just "explaining" their priorities better. Outcome benefited both teams. Names at least one specific person on the other team and what made them a partner.
- **4** — Specific collaboration, fair description of the other team, clear adaptation, outcome that worked.
- **3** — Specific situation but framing leans toward "our team was trying to get their team to do X." Some adaptation, OK outcome.
- **2** — Frames the other team as the obstacle. Adaptation is mostly about messaging. Escalation happened and was described as how it got resolved.
- **1** — Other team is the villain of the story. Escalation to management was the primary move. Or: cannot produce a cross-team example.

## Calibration examples

### Low (1-2)

> "We needed the platform team to enable a new feature flag service and they kept deprioritizing it. I finally had to get my VP to talk to their VP, and after that it got done in a week."

Other team framed as obstacle, escalation as the move, no adaptation, no partnership. Scores 1.

### Medium (3)

> "I needed the data team to build a pipeline for a project I was leading. I set up a weekly sync with their tech lead and wrote a clear spec, and we got it done about a month later than I wanted."

Specific, collaborative in form, but the framing is still "I needed them to build for me" with no real adaptation. Scores 3.

### High (4-5)

> "We needed the billing service to expose a new API endpoint for our migration. The billing team was mid-crunch on a SOX audit and their tech lead was skeptical about adding anything. I spent an hour with him understanding what the audit required, and I realized the endpoint I wanted was actually something they'd need for the audit too, just with a different response shape. We merged the two requirements, he put it on his sprint immediately because it now counted as audit work for his team, and we had it three weeks earlier than my original ask. I also learned he'd been pattern-matching my request to two earlier asks from us that turned out to be fire-and-forget — his skepticism wasn't about me, it was about our team's track record. I made a point of closing the loop with him after the migration shipped."

Specific, deep understanding of the other team's priorities, concrete adaptation that created mutual benefit, awareness of *why* the other team was skeptical, relationship-building. Scores 5.

## Probe library

- *other team as obstacle*: "What do you think was going on for them at the time? What were their priorities?"
- *escalation-first*: "Before the escalation — what did you try? What made you decide to escalate?"
- *no adaptation*: "Did you change anything about what you were asking for, based on what they needed?"
- *no named person*: "Who on the other team did you work most closely with? What made them a good partner?"

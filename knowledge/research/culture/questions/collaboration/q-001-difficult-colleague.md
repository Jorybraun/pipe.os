## Question

Tell me about a time you had to work closely with someone whose working style was very different from yours. What did you do to make the partnership work?

## Why we ask this

Collaboration skill is visible when styles clash, not when they align. Anyone can collaborate with people they naturally click with. The signal is whether the candidate can adapt their style, create bridges, and (critically) describe the other person's style with respect rather than contempt.

The question intentionally avoids the word "difficult" to allow for style difference without moral judgment, but candidates often turn it into a "difficult colleague" story anyway — which is itself informative.

## BARS rubric

- **5** — Names a specific colleague, describes their style fairly and without condescension, describes a concrete adaptation the candidate made (not just "we talked about it"), and describes the outcome. Shows evidence of the candidate thinking about how *they* landed on the other person, not just how the other person landed on them.
- **4** — Specific colleague, fair description, clear adaptation action, decent outcome. Less mutual awareness.
- **3** — Describes the situation but the other person is painted somewhat unfavorably. Adaptation is described but mild ("we set up weekly meetings").
- **2** — Other person is painted as the problem. Candidate's adaptation is minor. Outcome is framed as "getting through it."
- **1** — Describes the other person with contempt, frustration, or condescension. Candidate's only adaptation was enduring. Or: cannot produce an example.

## Calibration examples

### Low (1-2)

> "I had this one coworker who was just very slow to respond to anything. Wouldn't reply to Slack for hours, took forever in code review. Honestly I just started going around him when I needed things urgently, and eventually he moved to another team."

Contempt for the other person's style, "going around" is the opposite of collaboration adaptation, outcome is avoidance. Scores 1.

### Medium (3)

> "My tech lead was very detail-oriented and wanted to review every PR carefully, while I tend to move fast. I started writing more thorough PR descriptions so the reviews would go smoother, and we ended up shipping at a decent pace."

Specific, fair to the other person, concrete adaptation, decent outcome. Scores 3.

### High (4-5)

> "I paired with a staff engineer who processes ideas by talking them through out loud, whereas I think best in writing. At first I found our sessions exhausting because I felt pressured to have opinions in real time. After the second week I asked if I could take ten minutes after our meetings to write up my reactions in a doc, and then we'd have a second shorter meeting to decide. She liked it because she got my best thinking; I liked it because I wasn't drained. I also realized I'd been assuming verbal thinkers were wasting time, which was unfair — she was processing, not meandering. That framing change has made me a better pair partner with every verbal thinker since."

Specific, deep mutual awareness, concrete adaptation that worked for both people, explicit articulation of the candidate's own bias shift. Scores 5.

## Probe library

- *contempt for other person*: "What do you think was driving their style? What was it working for, from their side?"
- *no real adaptation*: "What did *you* specifically change about how you worked?"
- *one-sided framing*: "How do you think you came across to them during that time?"
- *vague outcome*: "How did you know the adaptation was working?"

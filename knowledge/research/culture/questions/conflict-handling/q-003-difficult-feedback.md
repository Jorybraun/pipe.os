## Question

Tell me about a time you had to give someone feedback that was hard to give. What was the situation, how did you approach it, and how did they respond?

## Why we ask this

Giving hard feedback is one of the clearest tests of whether a candidate will contribute to a healthy culture or silently let problems grow. The pattern we're looking for is: specific, timely, framed around behavior rather than identity, and followed up. The failure modes are: avoidance, delivering the feedback via a third party (manager, anonymous review), or delivering it harshly without regard for the relationship.

Important for any senior-and-above hire. Managers especially.

## BARS rubric

- **5** — Specific situation, specific person, describes the feedback they gave in actual language they used (or close to it). Framed around observable behavior and impact, not character. Delivered directly and timely. Followed up later to see how things went. Honest about their own discomfort.
- **4** — Specific, direct, behavioral, reasonable outcome. Less follow-up.
- **3** — Describes giving feedback but framing is more abstract ("I told them it wasn't working out"). Some discomfort acknowledged.
- **2** — Describes escalating the problem to a manager or delivering the feedback via a process (review cycle) rather than directly. Or: describes feedback that is actually criticism of character.
- **1** — Cannot produce an example. Or: describes avoiding the feedback and letting someone else give it. Or: describes delivering hard feedback with no regard for impact ("I just told them they were wrong").

## Calibration examples

### Low (1-2)

> "One of my reports wasn't pulling his weight. I tried to hint at it for a while but nothing changed, so I raised it with my manager and she handled it through the performance review process."

Avoided direct feedback, delegated to a process, no real engagement. Scores 1-2.

### Medium (3)

> "A junior engineer on my team kept submitting PRs without testing them locally, and they'd break CI. I told him a few times to test before pushing, and eventually he started doing it."

Specific, direct, behavioral — but the telling was shallow and there's no evidence of actual investment in the person. Scores 3.

### High (4-5)

> "I had a peer who dominated every design review we were in together. He'd interrupt people, including me, and the newer engineers had basically stopped speaking up. I noticed it was making our reviews worse — we were missing ideas from people who couldn't get a word in. I asked him to grab coffee and told him specifically: 'In the last three design reviews I counted you interrupting six, four, and seven times, and three people I respect have stopped proposing ideas in those meetings.' I said I knew he wasn't trying to shut people down but the effect was real. He was surprised and a little defensive at first, then genuinely upset — he hadn't noticed. We talked for about forty minutes. He asked me to give him a small signal when he was doing it in the moment, and I did for about a month. It took him maybe six weeks to self-correct. I checked back in at that point and also talked to two of the quieter engineers, who confirmed things felt different. The thing I'd do differently: I waited three weeks from first noticing to actually saying something. The conversation would have been easier for both of us if I'd had it sooner."

Specific, counted-evidence framing, behavioral not characterological, follow-through with both the person and the affected parties, explicit self-critique about delay. Scores 5.

## Probe library

- *avoidance / delegation*: "Did you give the feedback directly yourself, or did you go through someone else? Why?"
- *characterological*: "What were the specific behaviors you raised? Not how you characterized the person — the actions."
- *no follow-up*: "How did you know it landed? Did you check back in with them later?"
- *no discomfort*: "How did you feel going into that conversation? Was there any part of you that wanted to put it off?"
